## 1. Did nominal rates respond to recent inflation under commodity money?

Barsky, Robert B., "The Fisher Hypothesis and the Forecastability and Persistence of Inflation", NBER Working Paper 1927 (May 1986; Journal of Monetary Economics, January 1987). Opened: https://www.nber.org/papers/w1927 (abstract page) and https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w1927/w1927.pdf (full text, scanned with a text layer).

- Abstract: "Inflation evolved from essentially a white noise process in the pre-World War I years to a highly persistent, nonstationary ARIMA process in the post-1960 period."
- Abstract: "For the period 1860 to 1939, the simple correlation of the U.S. commercial paper rate with the contemporaneous inflation rate is -.17. The corresponding correlation for the period 1950 to 1979 is .71."
- Abstract: "neither high frequency nor low frequency movements in gold standard inflation rates were forecastable. Thus even if nominal rates responded fully to expected inflation, one would expect to find the zero coefficient obtained by Summers."
- Full text: "Inflation in this early period was very nearly white noise, so that the variance of anticipated inflation was very small relative to the variance of realized inflation."
- Full text: "It is striking that the data form the pre-World War I gold standard years show no sign of positive serial correlation." (sic, as printed)
- Full text: "In fact, nominal rates prior to 1913 followed highly persistent processes, although they were not a random walk (Mankiw and Miron, 1985)." The surrounding sentence says that if inflation is a martingale difference, the Fisherian model predicts white-noise nominal rates, and that interest-rate persistence can coexist with serially uncorrelated inflation.
- Reading: Barsky does not say rates ignored inflation. He says inflation was unforecastable, so the expected part was tiny and a missing correlation with realised inflation is what a full Fisher effect would also produce.

Barsky, Robert B. and Lawrence H. Summers, "Gibson's Paradox and the Gold Standard", NBER Working Paper 1680 (1985); Journal of Political Economy 96(3), 1988. Opened only the NBER abstract: https://www.nber.org/papers/w1680 . Quotes: "the observation that the price level and the nominal interest rate were positively correlated over long periods of economic history" and "its coincidence with the gold standard period". The abstract says the paradox is explained through the real return on capital and the relative price of gold. Full text not opened.

## 2. Theory: what the weight should be

- Lang, Harald, "Herman Wold on Optimal Properties of Exponentially Weighed Forecasts", Institute for Industrial Economics (IFN) Working Paper 179 (1987). Opened: https://www.ifn.se/media/e55pr1d5/wp179.pdf . Describes Muth (1960): income is a constant plus a random walk (permanent) plus white noise (transitory). Quote: "Under these assumptions Muth shows that the optimal forecast of Yt+1 (which is the same as the optimal measure of wt ) is the exponentially weighed sum". The weight is given as equation (3), a function only of the ratio of the variance of the random-walk increment to the variance of the transitory term. In the extracted text the formula is garbled, so I cannot reproduce it legibly.
- Sargent and Stachurski, QuantEcon lecture "Reverse Engineering a la Muth". Opened: https://python-advanced.quantecon.org/muth_kalman.html . Quotes: "an observable $y_t$ is the sum of an unobserved random walk $x_t$ and an IID shock" and the autoregressive coefficients "decline geometrically with decay rate" (1-K), where K in (0,1) is the steady-state Kalman gain. No closed-form variance formula on the page; the worked example has K of 0.1810.
- Muth, John F., "Optimal Properties of Exponentially Weighted Forecasts", Journal of the American Statistical Association 55(290), 1960, 299-306. The paper itself was not opened. Bibliographic details and the result were read only in the two sources above.
- Consequence that follows from the stated setup (a deduction, not a quote): if the permanent-part variance goes to zero, the signal-to-noise ratio goes to zero and the optimal weight goes to zero, so the best forecast is the long-run mean. Barsky's white-noise inflation is that case.
- Wikipedia, "Exponential smoothing", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_smoothing : "it can also be classified as an autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) (0,1,1) model". No weight formula there.

## 4. Rules by which the weight adapts

- Trigg and Leach (1967), "Exponential Smoothing with an Adaptive Response Rate", Operational Research Quarterly 18(1), 53-59. Paper not opened (no free copy found). Rule as stated in Wikipedia, "Tracking signal", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracking_signal : "One such approach is suggested by Trigg and Leach (1967), which requires the calculation of the tracking signal." and "The tracking signal is then used as the value of the smoothing constant for the next forecast." Formulas on the page, with beta the smoothing constant of the error averages: smoothed error E_t = beta e_t + (1 - beta) E_{t-1}; smoothed absolute error M_t = beta |e_t| + (1 - beta) M_{t-1}; tracking signal T_t = |E_t / M_t|. The page's rationale: a large signal suggests a shift, so a larger constant responds faster. If errors alternate in sign (pure noise), E stays near zero and so does the weight. Software documentation (Infor M3, https://docs.infor.com/m3dmp/2026.x/en-us/m3dmpolh/lto1746097698177.html) agrees only that alpha "is calculated based upon the smoothed forecast error and smoothed absolute forecast error".
- Marcet and Nicolini switching gain, equation (14) in the UPF excerpt above (text extracted, symbols garbled by font encoding, reading from context): the gain is the inverse of a counter alpha_t. Counter rule: alpha_t = alpha_{t-1} + 1 if the scaled prediction error is below a threshold nu, and alpha_t = alpha-bar otherwise. So the weight falls like 1/t (sample mean) while errors are small, and jumps to the fixed tracking value 1/alpha-bar after a large error. Quote: "as long as agents don't make large prediction errors, αt follows a least squares rule, but in periods where a large prediction error is detected, αt becomes a fixed positive value".
- Kesten (1958), "Accelerated Stochastic Approximation", Annals of Mathematical Statistics 29, 41-59. The paper and its abstract were not opened (Project Euclid page returned no text, Scilit returned 403). A web-search summary only says the step depends on the number of sign changes in successive differences. Rule not quoted from a source I opened.

## What this supports

Barsky's findings, for the gold standard period, support giving pre-modern commodity-money lenders little or no inflation pass-through when price changes are transient noise, while Muth's setup implies the weight should be near zero in that case and rise only when changes are persistent. Modern constant-gain figures that were found (0.05 and 0.018 per quarter, roughly 0.19 and 0.07 per year) are well below the current 0.5, and Trigg-Leach or Marcet-Nicolini give two simple published ways to let the weight rise only after persistent errors.
