Two observations that shape everything below.

1. A landholder group half exists already. Landholders are the employers of the labourer trade. When the founder or firms hire labourers away, `squeezed_employers` already produces "employers of labourer" with a loss equal to the hands priced out. What is missing is the landholder as a rent receiver (a crop price or import fall), not as an employer. Historically the employer channel is the first one (the Statute of Labourers of 1351, below), so that group can be named and labelled today.
2. The current pull is lost income over the territory's revenue. It has no term for how costly it is to organise, how concentrated the group is, or how close it sits to the state. Section 4 proposes those terms.

## 1. Per-group table

Columns: what it organised around, documented cases (with tags), what it pressed the state for or against, the simulator state that would measure size, income and stake, and the data still needed. "Buildable now" means a Sector source can be written against state that exists, with a labelled heuristic where noted.

### 1.1 Landholders

- **Organised around:** rents, labour supply and its price, tax exemption, and protection from imported food. Their stake is the rent a field earns and the cost of the hands that work it.
- **Cases:**
  - England 1351: the Statute of Labourers capped wages at the pre-plague level after the labour shortage, and the parliamentary Commons made it their "favourite child"; a wage cap served the smaller county landlord against labourers (snippet: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Labourers_1351 and a Wikipedia-derived commentary). It decayed because it could not compete with the market (snippet, same). The state's answer here is a price control, not a technique ban: a response kind the group code does not have.
  - England 1815 to 1846: the Corn Laws blocked wheat imports below a price and served landowners who dominated Parliament; industrialists and merchants opposed them through the Anti-Corn Law League founded in 1838; Peel repealed in 1846 after the Irish potato failure (snippet: nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/the-corn-laws, en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Corn_Law_League).
  - England 1830: threshing machines and the Swing riots; riot probability was higher in parishes with threshing machine advertisements, with machine breaking the sharpest, and lower where other employment softened the blow, higher where enclosure had already impoverished labourers (snippet: Caprettini and Voth, "Rage Against the Machines", papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2904322, jvoth.com). Here the landholders adopt and the labourers riot, so the same technique makes one group a winner and another a loser. A group source must give the sign of the effect by group, not only a loss.
  - Han China, second century AD: large estates absorbed peasant land and sheltered tenants and dependents to avoid tax and labour service, so the state lost revenue and manpower while the great families gained local independence (snippet: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_the_Han_dynasty and related pages). The group's strength grew as state capacity fell, the A and R mechanism in section 3.
  - Norse and Iceland: farmers who owned their land (bændr) and chieftains (goðar) whose authority depended on followers and not on territory; the tithe law of 1096 gave the church a claim on that income (snippet: worldhistory.org/Icelandic_Government, worldhistory.org/article/1905).
- **Pressed for or against:** wage caps and labour-service law, import duties, tax exemption, against techniques that raise the price of labour or bring cheaper competing food.
- **Simulator state:** the `rich` stratum and any stratum with `property_share` (members, own income); `squeezed_employers` for the labourer trade (employer channel, exists); `sim/world/land.py` rent per hectare (rent channel, static today); the share of the state's revenue that is land based (`state_revenue` split by source, to confirm); `own_plot` strata for smallholders.
- **Data needed:** a rent that moves with the crop price and the local labour price; a regional split of strata so that scope is not the whole country; for the employer channel, nothing new.
- **Buildable now:** the employer channel only. The rent channel needs the land model to carry a year-to-year rent first.

### 1.2 Organised workers

- **Organised around:** a trade whose real wage falls or whose work a machine takes; the skilled and the numerous differ in whether they can organise.
- **Cases:**
  - Nottinghamshire stocking frames, from March 1811, then Yorkshire croppers against shearing frames and gig mills; the Frame Breaking Act of February 1812 made frame breaking a capital offence (read: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddite, Wikipedia as last resort: the movement began in Arnold, Nottinghamshire, on 11 March 1811, Yorkshire croppers opposed shearing frames and gig mills, and Parliament made machine breaking a capital crime with the Destruction of Stocking Frames, etc. Act 1812; read: gedling.gov.uk/arnot-hill-park/sculptures/theluddites, the capital penalty was enacted in February 1812 and Byron's defence failed. Correction: the statute's own title is the Destruction of Stocking Frames, etc. Act 1812, and Frame Breaking Act is the popular name. victorianweb.org luddites was opened and dates the capital penalty as reinforced in 1811, which disagrees; the 1812 date is better supported). The state sided with the owners against the workers.
  - Lancashire 1779: spinners destroyed jennies and burned Arkwright's frames (snippet: mentalfloss.com spinning jenny, knittingtogether.org.uk; not opened). Not a state ban; a local riot.
  - Rome, Vespasian and the hoist: Suetonius reports that he rewarded an engineer who promised to move columns cheaply but would not use the device, saying he must let the poor commons earn their food (read: Suetonius, Vespasian 18, penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/12Caesars/Vespasian*.html: an engineer offered to move heavy columns to the Capitol cheaply and Vespasian declined the device, "You must let me feed my poor commons"). This is the state choosing not to adopt, for the sake of employment and order, not at the request of an organised group.
  - Rome, Trajan to Pliny (Letters 10.33 and 10.34): a requested firefighters' association was refused because associations in the province had become political clubs (snippet: ancientassociations.ku.dk, worldhistory.org/article/816). The ruler's suspicion of organisation itself limits how easily workers can form a group at all.
- **Pressed for or against:** a ban on the machine, a pay floor, relief, against hiring from outside the trade.
- **Simulator state:** strata with a trade (welfare against reference, `falling_incomes` exists); `labour_hours_coefficients_per_unit_output` and the trade's hours in each production entry, so the hours a new technique removes from a trade are computable; `sim/labour/` pool size and price factor per trade; the stratum's members and work share. Alternative employment is measurable as the unemployed pool of the trade against vacancies in other trades.
- **Data needed:** a per-trade measure of alternative employment (Caprettini and Voth give the sign: more outside options, less unrest); a per-civ tolerance of associations (see section 4, access term).
- **Buildable now:** a `labour_saving` source from production entries (section 2, channel B); the group size is the trade's hours removed divided by hours per person.

### 1.3 Owners and guilds (firm owners)

- **Organised around:** the entry barrier and the quality or apprenticeship rules that protect a rent. Historically the most successful blockers.
- **Cases:**
  - Mokyr: guilds and trade associations blocked countless cost-saving techniques, legally and illegally, from the Middle Ages (read: Mokyr, "Innovation and Its Enemies: The Economic and Political Roots of Technological Inertia", conference paper, New Delhi, January 1996, faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/jmokyr/Delhi.pdf, PDF pp. 28 to 30: in pre-modern urban Europe craft guilds "enforced and eventually froze the technological status quo", with similar phenomena in China, and examples include the printers' guild restraining a member from building an improved press in 1772 and the Viennese cabinetmakers' suit against Thonet in 1855. Correction: the phrase in that paper is "the purposeful resistance to new technology" (PDF p. 4), not "purposeful self-interested resistance"; the paper's thesis is also mixed, since it argues resistance can be a logical and even socially beneficial source of stability. The words "countless cost-saving techniques, legally and illegally" were not found in it and stay snippet).
  - Ogilvie: craft guilds adversely affected quality, skills and innovation, accepted innovation only when it served members, and rent seeking imposed deadweight losses; Epstein disputes the generalisation (read: Ogilvie, "Guilds, efficiency, and social capital: evidence from German proto-industry", Economic History Review 57(2), 2004, pp. 286 to 333, full text at bollettinoadapt.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/EcHR-2004-guilds.pdf, and the working paper version CESifo 820 (2002). Pages 314 to 316 confirm that guilds tolerated innovations they judged harmless and blocked others, and that guilds supplied the social capital that lowered the cost of collective action for established producers, with Wurttemberg worsted examples from 1619 to 1775; the abstract finds little evidence that guilds corrected market failures in quality, training or innovation. Correction: the words deadweight losses were not found in the article and stay unverified, and the eh.net review could not be opened. Disputed: Ogilvie against the "rehabilitation" school of Epstein, Pfister and Reith, which holds that guilds did not block practical innovation or even encouraged it; Ogilvie rebuts its three arguments on those pages. Epstein's own paper was not opened). Treat the size of the guild effect as contested; the simulator should let it be an outcome of strength and state access, not a constant.
  - Roman shipowners (navicularii): emperors granted exemptions and privileges to shippers who carried the annona grain, and Claudius took on the winter risk himself to draw them in (read: romanports.org/en/articles/human-interest/657-the-roman-shipowners.html, a web article with no named scholar: exemptions from public obligations for shippers on the annona contracts, Claudius covering damage to draw winter shipping, minimum ship size rising over time). Here the state and a group traded service for privilege, and the group's privilege rose with the state's dependence on it, not with a lobbying loss. By the fourth century the membership was bound by law (read, same page: mandatory membership of the shippers' corpora by the fourth century).
  - Han salt and iron: the 81 BC court debate between the state's "modernists" who wanted to keep the monopolies and funds for the northern wars, and the reformist scholars who wanted them abolished (read: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yantie_lun, Wikipedia as last resort: modernists defended the monopolies as a check on wealthy merchants and as funding for military campaigns, reformists opposed them on Confucian grounds; the modernists prevailed at first and only the liquor monopoly was abolished; the Eastern Han returned to private salt and iron production while keeping the coinage monopoly. Correction: the monopolies were also briefly lifted from 44 to 41 BC and resumed before the Eastern Han ended them). The monopoly itself is the state in the role of incumbent, protecting a revenue line. In the Eastern Han the monopolies were mostly repealed except for coinage (same page).
- **Simulator state:** `Firm.record.last_margin` and `exited_year` (Complaint 311); firms in a category (`sim.actor_concerns_in(category)`); `founder_share` of sellers in `goods_categories`; the state's own monopoly revenue as a budget source (to confirm).
- **Data needed:** a per-civ legal form of association (guild charter, collegium licence, state monopoly, none); who may hold which privilege.
- **Buildable now:** a margin-erosion group from firm records. The legal form is data not engine.

### 1.4 Clergy

- **Organised around:** a monopoly on literacy and copying, a tithe or land base, and moral authority over credit and the calendar.
- **Cases:**
  - Trithemius, abbot of Sponheim, wrote De laude scriptorum in 1492 defending hand copying against the press, while seeing what printing offered scholars (snippet: purplemotes.net/2012/12/23/trithemius-printing-scribes-reason, hmml.org). A defence of income and devotion, not a ban.
  - Third Lateran Council 1179: impenitent moneylenders refused the altar, absolution and Christian burial (read: Third Lateran Council 1179, canon 25, clerus.org/bibliaclerusonline/en/bsp.htm: "notorious usurers should not be admitted to communion of the altar or receive christian burial if they die in this sin". Correction: the canon names notorious usurers, not impenitent moneylenders, and the text opened does not mention absolution). Church law against interest taxed a technique (lending) without any state petition.
  - Ottoman Empire: Bayezid II is reported to have prohibited printing in Arabic script around 1483 to 1485 on pain of death, a fatwa of 1508 allowed printing for non-Muslims and not Muslims, and Ibrahim Muteferrika's 1727 press was licensed for secular works and not religious ones, opposed by calligraphers and part of the ulama (read, secondary: roger-pearse.com/weblog/2009/03/07/printing-banned-by-islam, a blog post summarising Schwartz's study; the 1508 fatwa and the calligraphers' opposition to Muteferrika were not found there and stay snippet: historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=330). Disputed, both sides named: the traditional account, first published by Andre Thevet in 1584, that Bayezid II and Selim I banned printing on pain of death, against the finding that no firman of either sultan survives, that Marsigli denied a prohibition, and that the 1727 firman for Muteferrika allowed secular works and excepted religious law, Koranic exegesis, hadith and theology, a licensing limit and not a general ban. The death penalty claim is widely repeated and also disputed; verify before use. The pattern, a scribal trade and a religious body acting together and a state allowing the technique for those outside the incumbents' sphere, is the useful part.
  - Iceland: the tithe law of 1096 (snippet above); Han and later monasteries (the Northern Wei equal-field and monasteries event in the Han file).
- **Simulator state:** none directly. There is no priest trade and no church stratum. The nearest proxies are the `scribe` and `scholar` trades and literacy by stratum; hazards name the church events but only as dated shocks. The `printing` goods category exists, so a scribe group could be measured against it.
- **Data needed:** an institution actor (church) with a land and tithe base, an estate of literate staff, and a rule on lending. This is the largest missing piece and the clearest mod candidate (owner decision on 311).
- **Buildable now:** no. A proxy group of scribes against the printing category is buildable (section 1.6).

### 1.5 Military

- **Organised around:** pay, privilege, and the mode of fighting that gives the corps its standing.
- **Cases:**
  - Ottoman Janissaries opposed Selim III's reforms, which tried to keep them and then built a replacement army (Nizam-i Cedid, 1797), and in 1807 Janissaries and urban allies attacked the new troops, deposed and later killed him (snippet: en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Selim_III, ebsco.com research starter). A military corps that is also a political and urban faction and defends its place against a technique-bearing rival.
  - Tokugawa Japan: Perrin's account that firearms were effectively given up after the Sengoku period, with a ruling warrior class valuing the sword; Japanese historians dispute that the gun was ever really abandoned (read: Enomoto, "Giving Up the Gun? Disputed, Perrin against the Japanese historians Enomoto cites; use only as a warning that a state may suppress a technique that undermines its warrior class.
  - Rome: Pliny and Trajan above also shows the emperor's fear of armed or organised bodies in the provinces; the soldiers' pay was a major state line (recalled).
  - Austria and Russia in the 19th century: officials and rulers opposed railways and industry; the Russian official Kankrin is quoted as seeing railways as encouraging "socially dangerous mobility" (snippet: Acemoglu and Robinson, via the Journal of Economic History review of Economic Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy; original text unread).
- **Simulator state:** `record.army` and `army_wanted()`, the soldier trade, the army line's need, unfunded and funded share (`sim/agents/budget.py`), `military_leverage()` (the founder's strength, not the state's), `armour` and `implements_and_arms` need goods.
- **Data needed:** a measure of whether a technique changes the value of the soldiers' own skill, such as a weapon that cuts hours per unit of force in the army line's input mix; the rate of arrears and its link to unrest (Complaint 266 notes there is no mutiny link).
- **Buildable now:** an arrears group (soldiers unpaid when the army line is underfunded; stake is the unfunded amount, size is the army). This is a grievance against the state, not against a technique, and uses existing state. A substitution link (a weapon technique that cuts army labour hours) needs the labour-saving source of section 2.

## 2. The substitution rule: who loses when a technique spreads, without per-technique authoring

The goal is a relation computed from what the data already says, so no technique needs an `incumbents` list. The inputs are production entries (outputs, inputs, labour hours by trade, `requires_node`), the `satisfies` weights of goods in `data/world/needs.json`, goods categories, and the goods market. The rule has three channels and one reach step.

Let a technique T be a tech node. Its entries are the production entries with `requires_node` equal to T (the same lookup as `supply.materials_made_by`). Each entry has an output good and, if the good appears in `needs.json`, a set of needs it satisfies with an effectiveness per unit.

**Channel A, product substitution (a new good replaces an old one in a need).** Group the goods by the need they satisfy. When T makes a good g and g's effectiveness-adjusted price is below incumbent good h in the same need, demand moves from h to g by the existing constant elasticity of substitution (`NEED_SUBSTITUTION_ELASTICITY`). The sales h loses are the cross-price effect the market clearing already computes for the founder's own sales (`displaced_by_founder_tonnes`); the generalisation is to compute it for every good in the need, not only commodities the founder sells. The loser group is the producers of h: the strata whose trade appears in h's own production entries, weighted by each entry's hours (the same trade weights as `goods_sectors`, but taken from `labour_hours` of the entry, not the node `lab` heuristic). No per-technique authoring: the incumbent is whatever else answers the need.

**Channel B, process substitution (the same good, fewer hands).** When T has an entry whose output is the same material as an incumbent entry (not gated by T) and with lower `labour_hours` for a trade, the hours difference per unit, times the quantity T's route comes to produce, is the work that trade loses. The loser group is that trade's stratum, and it loses even when no price falls for consumers (the Luddite and threshing-machine cases: the product is the same). The trade's loss is net of what the trade's hours rise elsewhere in the same entry (machinists replacing croppers), so the rule gives both a loser and, with a sign flip, a winner.

**Channel C, input substitution (a different input mix).** When T's entry consumes different inputs from the incumbent entry for the same output (coal for charcoal, glass for horn), the suppliers of the input whose quantity falls are the losers, and the `squeezed_employers` and `displaced_producers` sources already cover their loss once the input's market clears at a lower quantity.

**Reach step (Complaint 313, the prohibition).** A group that lost on a subject (commodity, category, trade) asks the state to forbid the nodes that cause it. Today the link is "node makes a commodity of the subject" or "node shares the category". The substitution rule gives a better link: node N is reached by a group if any entry gated by N satisfies a need that the group's good also satisfies (channel A), or has lower `labour_hours` for the group's trade on a shared output (channel B), or changes the input mix of the group's good (channel C). The loser's subject is thereby derived from the data, and a concern that sells a good into a need appears the same way through the goods market. Protection should only bargain a prohibition down rather than lift it outright unless the owner decides otherwise (313's open decision).

What this rule cannot see: status or doctrine losers (academics, clergy on moral grounds), organisational substitutes (a joint stock company replacing a guild), and techniques that change the military value of a skill. Those need an institution layer, not a recipe comparison.

Practical caution: `satisfies` appears in the needs file for a limited number of goods, and the `_SCHEMA.md` text says production entries may also carry `satisfies` and `supply_per_year`. How many production entries lack a need link was not measured here; `python3 sim/simulator.py validate` or a short script over `data/production/` would count them before a build is sized.

## 3. Theory and what it asks of the strength rule

- **Olson, The Logic of Collective Action (1965; read, secondary only: britannica.com/biography/Mancur-Olson, which confirms that coercion or another device is needed for a group to act in its common interest, that selective incentives overcome free riding, and that small cohesive groups act more easily; adambrown.info/p/notes/olson_the_logic_of_collective_action, reader notes on free riding in latent groups and on smaller stakeholders exploiting larger ones. The book was not opened, so page numbers are missing, and the claim that concentrated minorities dominate diffuse majorities stays recalled; the reader notes state the exploitation as small stakeholders against large ones):** concentrated interests with a large per-member stake organise more easily than diffuse ones; the free rider problem grows with group size; selective incentives or coercion are needed for large latent groups; small concentrated minorities can dominate diffuse majorities. Implication: the beneficiaries of a technique (consumers) are diffuse and do not petition, while the incumbents (a few guild masters) do. The pull should rise with stake per member and fall with group size, unless an institution supplies selective incentives (a guild, a collegium, a church).
- **Acemoglu and Robinson, "Political Losers as a Barrier to Economic Development", AER 2000, and Economic Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy (2006) (read: AER 90(2), May 2000, pp. 126 to 130, aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.90.2.126, bibliographic page only with no abstract, so the claim that political losers matter more than economic losers stays snippet. Correction: the non-monotonic result and the Russia and Austria-Hungary illustration belong to Acemoglu and Robinson, "Economic Backwardness in Political Perspective", American Political Science Review 100(1), February 2006, pp. 115 to 131, read at cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/economic-backwardness-in-political-perspective/7DE0FEDD01FA04387AB1F4689CF7944B: elites are unlikely to block with high political competition or when highly entrenched, block when competition is limited and their power is threatened, and block more when stakes such as land rents are higher; external threats reduce blocking. I could not confirm that the 2000 AER paper carries that result):** political losers, elites whose power would be eroded, block growth more than economic losers do; the relation between blocking and political competition is non-monotonic, with elites blocking when competition is limited and their power is threatened but not when they are entrenched or when competition is strong. The Russian and Austrian opposition to railways is the illustration (see 1.5). Implication: success should depend on whether the technique erodes the group's hold on state office or tax base, not only its income, and should be low when the group is so entrenched that it does not feel threatened and when competition is high.
- **Mokyr, "Technological inertia in economic history", Journal of Economic History 1992, and The Gifts of Athena (read: the 1992 abstract at ideas.repec.org and the 1996 conference paper, see 1.2 and 1.5; The Gifts of Athena was not opened. The claim that resistance succeeds when the state is weak or captured and fails when exit is possible was not found in the abstract and stays snippet; the 1996 paper does argue that competition between neighbouring states promotes change, so the exit point is plausible):** resistance comes from vested interests and from intellectuals; it succeeds when the state is weak or captured and fails when the state has other uses for the technology (military need, revenue) or when exit is possible by moving to another jurisdiction. Guilds are the central historical example. Implication: the state's own interest in the technique (the existing `state_interest`) already spares a node, and a competing jurisdiction (foreign economies exist in `sim/engine/foreign_economies.py`) is an exit that should reduce a ban's effect.
- **Caprettini and Voth (snippet, 1.1):** unrest tracks the technique's local spread and falls with alternative employment and rises with prior impoverishment. Implication: the workers' grievance should be weighted by outside options and prior welfare, both of which strata and the labour pool carry.

### The strength rule

Today `pull = 1 - exp(-lost_income / revenue / scale)`. A rule that keeps this shape and adds the factors the theory names, each a measured quantity (the weights are labelled temporary heuristics per CLAUDE.md 4.4):

```
effective_stake = lost_income_share_of_state_revenue
                  * organisation_factor
                  * access_factor
pull = 1 - exp(-effective_stake / pull_scale)
```

- `organisation_factor` (Olson): rises with income lost per member (stake over the cost of acting) and falls as members grow, unless the group carries an institutional form (guild, collegium, church) that supplies selective incentives. Measure: lost income per member over the labourer wage (already computed as `members = lost / wage` for materials, which is the wrong direction for this: the member count should come from the actual body, not be backed out from the loss), and the share of members in a single town (concentration, from regional strata). The institutional form is civ data.
- `access_factor` (Acemoglu and Robinson, Mokyr): rises with the group's share of the offices and revenue the state depends on (landholders and officials as the state's tax base and its staff), and with the state's dependence on the group's service (the Roman shippers carrying grain). Measure: the group's trade or stratum share of the state's budget lines as staff or as the revenue source; `officials_kept`, the army line, `property_share` strata.
- The state's response keeps `state_capacity` and the deficit test and adds two terms: the counter-weight of those who gain (the founder, firms and the state's own revenue, which already exists as `state_interest`), and the entrenchment non-monotonicity: a group so strong that no one challenges it asks for less because it is not threatened.

Success should be a relation, not a fixed answer: the state forbids when `pull * capacity` exceeds the state's own forgone gain from the technique (`state_interest`, plus tax revenue the technique would raise), is not in deficit, and no exit exists for the technique. This replaces the fixed `GROUP_BAN_PULL` line with a comparison of two measured amounts. That is a design change worth an owner decision before building.

## 4. Staged build plan with relationship tests

Tests assert relationships between runs, never dated events or magnitudes (CLAUDE.md 4.2, 6). Each stage is independent and mod-friendly: a stage adds a Sector source behind the existing `Sector` interface and ships data, not engine branches (CLAUDE.md 4.7).

**Stage 0 (no behaviour change).** Label the temporary heuristics the existing groups use that this report relies on (the trade weights by node hours, members backed out of the loss). Add the per-civ association tolerance field to the civilisation files as data (none, licensed, chartered, state monopoly), sourced to the cases above.
- Test: validate passes; fingerprint unchanged.

**Stage 1: channel B, labour saving (workers, scribes, soldiers).** A `labour_saving` source reading the hours difference between entries for the same output.
- Relationship tests: a group exists for a trade only when a technique's entry for the same output uses fewer hours of that trade than a non-gated entry; the group's lost income rises monotonically with the quantity the new route produces; a technique with equal hours produces no group; the sign flips (a winning trade) when hours of another trade rise, and no group forms for a winner.
- Cross-check against Caprettini and Voth: with more unemployed capacity in other trades the group's pull is lower.

**Stage 2: channel A, product substitution, and society-side producers (Complaint 312).** Route concern output through market flows; compute displaced sales for every good in a need.
- Relationship tests: a founder who sells into a need with no incumbent displaces nobody and no group forms; with an incumbent in the same need the displaced quantity rises with the founder's share and falls as the substitution elasticity falls; a founder who keeps what he makes displaces less than one who sells it.

**Stage 3: reach step (Complaint 313).** Replace gate-based reach with the substitution reach; make protection bargain a prohibition down in proportion to the group's pull, with the owner's decision on whether it can lift it outright.
- Relationship tests: the number of nodes a prohibition covers is greater than the gates-only count for the same group; a node the state values is still spared; raising protection lowers the prohibition's effect monotonically; with an unforbidden exit (a foreign market) the same group's ban lowers the technique's local cost less.

**Stage 4: strength rule (section 3).** Add `organisation_factor` and `access_factor` behind labelled constants.
- Relationship tests: with a fixed total loss, a group of fewer members with the same total loss has greater pull than one of many; a group with an institutional form has greater pull than the same group without; a group whose trade supplies the state's staff has greater pull; a state in deficit pays rather than bans.

**Stage 5: groups on existing state.** Arrears groups for soldiers and officials (army and administration lines' unfunded share), a relief group for the poor stratum with a dole answer, a landholder employer group naming the labourer employers.
- Relationship tests: unfunded share of the army line above zero produces a soldier group, and funding it removes the group; a staple price rise raises the poor's group size and the dole claim; hiring labourers away raises the landholders' employer loss.

**Stage 6: landholder rent.** Carry a year-to-year rent in `sim/world/land.py` that follows the crop price and the hands a farm can hire.
- Relationship tests: a fall in the crop price lowers rent; an increase in the farm wage lowers rent; with imports, the landholder group's loss rises with the import share; a threshing-type technique raises landholder income and lowers labourer income.

**Stage 7 (mod, per the owner decision).** Clergy, academics and any institution with a status or doctrine stake, as a mod that adds an institution actor and a church land and tithe base, using the same Sector interface.

## 5. Open questions

1. How many production entries carry a need link or share an output with another entry? The substitution rule is only as strong as that coverage. Count with a short script over `data/production/` before sizing stages 1 to 3 (not run here).
2. The state's response kinds: group code knows compensate and ban only. The Statute of Labourers is a price control, the Corn Laws a tariff, Vespasian a refusal to adopt, the Ottoman case a ban with a carve-out for outsiders. Should the response set be data (a list of kinds a state of a given type uses) and who chooses?
3. Protection and prohibition (313): should protection lift a prohibition that the state made at an organised group's request, or only bargain it down as it does a levy?
4. Where does the group's membership come from for strata that are national? A regional split of strata (urban against rural) is needed for concentration, scope and the urban poor.
5. A winner's side: the same technique makes landholders (threshing) and employers (cheap labour) winners and labourers losers. Should winners form groups that petition for the technique (the Anti-Corn Law League is the case) and, if so, does the state weigh them against the losers by the same strength rule?
6. Is `property_share` (a fixed share of output) acceptable as the landholder's income until the rent exists, or should the `falling_incomes` source ignore property strata for technique blame until it moves on its own?
7. Political competition: A and R's non-monotonic result needs a measure of how contested the state is. The strata file has `rises_to` and `falls_to` mobility but no political competition variable, and the conditional events research (Complaint 266) lists legitimacy and elite factionalism as not modelled. Without it, the entrenchment term cannot be built, and the strength rule above is Olson only.
8. How much of this is a mod versus core? The owner decision on 311, 312 and 313 says mod and deferred. The core should ship the Sector interface, the substitution reach and the strength rule, with groups as data; clergy, academics and any status-based group should be mod content.
9. Several Ottoman and Tokugawa claims are disputed (the death penalty for printing, the Japanese abandonment of the gun). They are used only as examples of a pattern, but a verifier should open them before any is cited in game text.
