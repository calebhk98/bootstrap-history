## 1. Levels and variation

### 1a. Rome and Roman Egypt

1. The unit "centesimae usurae" is one percent a month, 12 percent a year.
   Source: https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0063%3Aentry%3Dfenus-cn (Smith, Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities, FENUS).
   Quote: "centesimae usurae, inasmuch as at this rate of interest there was paid in a hundred months a sum equal to the whole principal. Hence binae centesimae = 24 per cent., and quaternae centesimae =48 per cent."

2. Late Republic, in Rome: low but fluctuating; a sudden rise.
   Same source. Quote: "In Rome itself, at the same period, the accumulation of capital brought about a reasonably low though fluctuating rate of interest on good business; Cicero mentions a sudden rise from 4 per cent. to 8 ( ad Att. 4.15)."
   Caveat: secondary (1890 dictionary). I did not open the letter itself.

3. Late Republic, provinces: a 48 percent demand was backed by force or a magistrate.
   Same source. Quote: "the virtuous Brutus, acting of course through an intermediary and keeping behind the scenes, tried to get 48 per cent. out of the Cyprians, and plagued Cicero, then proconsul of Cilicia, to put on the screw for him." Also "Verres ... made 24 per cent. out of the public money".

4. Early empire, in Italy: 5 percent was the "modest" rate, 11 percent was "sweating"; charitable funds lent at 5 percent and 2.5 percent.
   Same source. Quote: "Under the early empire we find the now familiar expression 'a modest 5 per cent.,' while 11 per cent savours of 'sweating' ... The endowment of the alimentarii at Veleia was invested on mortgage at 5 per cent.; that of another similar charity, the alimentarii Baebiani in Liguria, at only 2 1/2".

5. A cash glut moved the rate by a factor of three in one episode (Rome, 29 BC triumph of Octavian).
   Source: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/51*.html (Dio 51.21, Loeb translation).
   Quote: "So vast an amount of money, in fact, circulated through all parts of the city alike, that the price of goods rose and loans for which the borrower had been glad to pay twelve per cent. could now be had for one third that rate."
   This is the cleanest observed case in the sources of plentiful savings (booty from Egypt) pushing the rate down, and it stopped near 4 percent, not zero.

6. Roman Egypt, usual rate: 12 percent, very stable.
   Source: https://rosetta.bham.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Kelly_Review-of-Lerouxel_Rosetta22.pdf (Kelly's review of Lerouxel, PDF text extracted).
   Quote: "according to his data there was almost no diachronic volatility in interest rate structures". Also: "Lerouxel perhaps overstates the universality and invariability of the typical 12% interest rate in Roman Egypt ... Interest rates certainly did differ according to the size and nature of the transaction with, for instance, very high interest rates applying in certain pawnbroker loans."
   The review gives no number for pawnbroker loans.

7. Late antique Egypt (400 to 700 CE), year-to-year stability and then dispersion.
   Source: https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:5558d838-ffd4-4671-a801-0073fa017210/files/m29cc08c46ad5720b1aa41ce9c0f9896f (Buchanan, Oxford DPhil 2015, "Debt in Late Antique Egypt").
   Quote: "Between 400 and 528, interest rates for cash debts were very stable at twelve per cent. After 528, they range from six per cent to fifty per cent. Most of the cash debts with very high interest rates had other unique factors. Even with these cases removed from consideration, the rate of interest fluctuated between 4.2 and 16.6 per cent, with a steady baseline rate of twelve to 12.5 per cent". Also: "Actual interest rates varied widely, even in the same period and archive." And Justinian "reduced interest rates from the long-standing twelve per cent per year to six per cent for most individuals in 528. The interest rate for bankers was set at eight per cent", which "was not, however, widely adopted".
   Also a papyrologist's note: "The usual rate of interest was 12 % per annum, see CPR VII pp. 162-3 Exkurs 5" (https://www.uni-koeln.de/phil-fak/ifa/zpe/downloads/2000/129pdf/129185.pdf, ZPE 129, p. 186).

8. History Today "Roman Loans" (Paul Kelly, Dec 2017): snippet only, via search. The page itself returned HTTP 403 to me twice, so not opened. The snippet said it concerns Roman Egypt, a 12 percent annual limit on cash loans (reduced from a 24 percent maximum before the Roman conquest, snippet only), "many examples of loans at higher rates of interest, especially for smaller sums". URL: https://www.historytoday.com/history-matters/roman-loans

### 1b. England around 1300 (best data of any pre-modern economy I found)

9. Return on capital, decade medians from 384 rent-charge conveyances: stable near 10 percent through 1200 to 1290, then lower.
   Source: https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/ecn110a/readings/chapter9.pdf (Clark, Econ 110A chapter 9, Table 4, which cites Clark's "The Cost of Capital").
   Table 4 median return by decade: 1170 9.2; 1180 10.5; 1190 9.7; 1200 8.9; 1210 10.5; 1220 10.0; 1230 10.2; 1240 10.0; 1250 10.0; 1260 10.0; 1270 10.7; 1280 10.8; 1290 10.0; 1300 8.2; 1310 8.0 (only 2 transfers); 1340 7.5 (1 transfer); 1370 5.0 (1 transfer).
   So for about a century the decade median moved by less than 1 point; thin samples after 1300.

10. Period averages, rent charges (Table 2) and land rent over price (Table 3), same source.
    Rent charges, England: pre-1351 10.6; 1351-1400 4.5; 1451-1500 4.0; 1551-1600 6.0; 1751-1800 4.0. France pre-1351 11.0. Germany pre-1351 10.2.
    Land (rent/price), England: pre-1351 9.7; 1351-1400 9.4; 1401-1450 5.6; 1451-1500 5.0; 1701-1750 4.3; 1751-1800 3.6.
    Note the two series differ by about 5 points in 1351 to 1400 and the land series falls later than the rent-charge series.

11. Clark's abstract of the Black Death paper.
    Source: https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/papers/black1.pdf
    Quote: "the return on capital did fall from 10% around 1300 to about 5% by 1400, the biggest change in English history. But this decline seemingly began around 1300, long before the Black Death".
    Dispersion within a decade: "the range of years purchase ... ranging from 2 to 53. But almost half the transactions involved a years purchase of between 9 and 11." Years purchase is the inverse of the rate, so single deals ranged from 50 percent to under 2 percent while the median held at 10.

12. Clark's definitions, and why the series is noisy early.
    Source: https://faculty.econ.ucdavis.edu/faculty/gclark/papers/Macroagg2009.pdf (Clark, Macroeconomic Aggregates for England, section 14).
    Quote: "Rent charges were perpetual fixed obligations secured by land or houses ... The ratio of the sum paid per year to the price of such a rent charge gives the interest rate for another very low risk asset". Figure note: "For the years before 1350 the land returns are the moving average of 3 decades because in these early years this measure is very noisy." Clark adds a 5 percent risk premium for non-farm capital ("the interest cost is the risk free interest rate plus a premium for risk of 5%").

13. Independent confirmation of the definition and data in a 2025 QJE paper.
    Source: https://eml.berkeley.edu/~enakamura/papers/malthus.pdf (Bouscasse, Nakamura, Steinsson, QJE 140(2)).
    Quote: "The rate of return on agricultural land is measured as R/P, where R is the rent and P is the price of a piece of land. 'Rent charges' ... were yields on perpetual nominal debt obligations secured by land or buildings (i.e., a collateralized loan)."
    This is the form a simulator can compute: rent over price from the land market.

14. England, the rate at the highest legal ceiling: Schmelzing, as quoted.
    Source: https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/working-paper/2020/eight-centuries-of-global-real-interest-rates-r-g-and-the-suprasecular-decline-1311-2018 (Schmelzing, BoE Staff Working Paper 845).
    Quote: "In the early 14th century, double-digit nominal rates north of 15% are the norm across advanced economies, though England, where King Henry III's interest rate ceiling of 45% was enforced, stands out with higher averages (Bond 1840, 225f.)." I did not check Bond 1840; the 45 percent ceiling and the date are the author's statement, and I regard the figure as doubtful until checked.

### 1c. Other economies with annual data

15. Genoa and Venice public debt, annual or period yields (Fratianni and Spinelli, Table 1, text extracted from PDF so column placement is my reading).
    Source: https://people.bu.edu/chamley/HSFref/FratianniSpinelliEREH06.pdf
    Genoa 1382-85 average 8.83; Venice 1382-85 14.85; Genoa 1386-1407 average 7.03; Genoa 1549 3.87, 1552 3.87, 1560 3.66, 1574 3.86, 1576 2.79. The Holland column (same table) shows 6.25 in 1549, 8.33 in 1552, 20.0 in 1574, 8.33 in 1576.
    So Genoa's San Giorgio yield moved by well under 1 point year to year in 1549 to 1574, while a besieged Holland moved by 12 points in two years.

16. Real-rate volatility, sovereign safe assets, 1311 to 2018 (Schmelzing, same BoE paper).
    Quote: "the average standard deviation for safe asset provider real rates stands at 3.92% (30-year centred average basis); over a 200-year horizon (1818-2018) it is 4.72%; while the all-time level is 10.4%." And: "real rate volatility has exhibited a long downward trend since the early 1500s." Also "the century-average safe real rate peaked in the 15th century at 9.1%, and declined to 6.1% in the 16th century, followed by 4.6% in the 17th". Caveat: real rates include price shocks, so this overstates the volatility of the nominal lending rate.
    Also: Italian primary and secondary yields differ by under a point: "the absolute difference between yearly means of average primary yields at issue divided by secondary market rates is less than one percent" (same paper).

Summary for the sim: in the cases with data, the structure of rates is sticky over years (Egypt 12 percent for 128 years, English rent charges within a point per decade for a century, Genoa within a point for 25 years). Large moves are rare and have a visible cause (a cash influx, a siege, a political change). Scatter between individual deals in one year is large.

## 2. What set the floor when savings were plentiful

Evidence is thinner than the first pass implied. What I read:

A. Return on land is the anchor in the English data, and the rent-over-price ratio is the observable. Clark's own method treats the land rent/price ratio and the rent-charge yield as two measures of the same rate (sources 10, 12). But the relation is not a one-way floor: Schmelzing reports that in the 14th century land returns were "structurally more 'risky' than safe financial assets", citing Genoa where "the investment in government debt was preferred to the investment in housing" and a Venetian diarist: "they preferred to invest their money in the Monte Nuovo rather than in estates" (same BoE paper). So land is one alternative, and its yield net of risk and management could sit above or below the loan rate. A simulator should let savers compare, not assume land always sets the floor.

B. Opportunity cost in trade and farming, as an explanation of why rates stayed high where capital was not scarce. Source: Smith's dictionary (Perseus link above), on the Greeks: "This high rate does not appear to have been caused by any scarcity of money, but by the unwillingness of those who had capital to lend it out at a low rate of interest, since they could use it themselves to more advantage in trade, or, owing to the cheapness of slave labour, in agriculture; hence even the interest on land and house property is proportionately high." Secondary, 1890, and about Greece. It states the mechanism clearly but is not evidence about Rome.

C. Default and political risk. Clark's chapter 9: "Rates of return from lending to princes and kings were often much higher than this. But these rates included a substantial premium for the danger that the lender would default, as they often did." And: "The perpetuities owned by the crown sold in 1650 for on average implied rate of return of 11.2%, at a time when private perpetuities yielded a return of about 5.5%." Same chapter 9 PDF. Clark also tests whether risk of death or turmoil explains the 1300 to 1650 gap and finds only part: "at maximum 0.5% - 2% of the higher interest rate of the pre-industrial period could be accounted for by the risks of death" (chapter 9).
   Schmelzing on enforcement: Italian city lenders were the elite, "the system worked because it aligned the creditors' and debtors' incentives" (quoting Epstein 2000), which is why those cities had low yields.

D. Time preference and growth. Clark: the return on capital "should depend on three things only in the long run: the time preference of individuals ... the rate of growth of incomes per person ... and the security of investments" (black1.pdf). In chapter 9 he says the high pre-1349 rates "may have resulted from myopic preferences by medieval consumers" and describes selection of patient types as one possible cause, adding that it is unresolved ("the evidence is ambiguous"). So time preference is a hypothesis in Clark, not a measured number.

E. Hoarding. Tacitus (Annals 6.17, AD 33) records the opposite of a hoard that stays idle: after the law was invoked, "the usurers had hoarded up all their money for buying land", and the rush to sell land caused prices to fall (https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Annals_(Tacitus)/Book_6). Coin was small relative to wealth: Scheidel cites Gregory King as showing coinage "less than 4 percent of all wealth in England near the end of the seventeenth century" (https://zenodo.org/records/3458611, preprint PDF). No source found that treats hoarding as a floor on loan rates.

F. Cost of running loans (monitoring, notaries, fixed cost per loan). No source found. Only indirect: small borrowers paid more (History Today snippet, Kelly's review of Lerouxel: rates "did differ according to the size and nature of the transaction"). Not a cost measure.

G. Lender profit spread. Snippet only, search results: financiers profited from the difference between interest paid to savers and charged to borrowers, in Genoa. No figure found. No source found for Roman deposit rates.

H. What stopped a glut from taking the rate to zero. In the one clear case (Dio 51.21) the rate fell to a third of its prior level, to 4 percent, and the first-pass claim of a zero floor is not supported by any source I read. Pre-modern series I read never show a zero or negative nominal rate. Schmelzing counts negative real rates, but "not a single instance occurred on the global level prior to" the 17th century (BoE paper), and those are real rates after inflation.

## 3. How lenders fared when they lent too cheaply or too riskily

17. Bardi and Peruzzi (Florence, 1340s). The conventional account is lending to Edward III. Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jechis/v50y1990i01p149-162_03.html (Hunt, JEH 50(1), abstract). Quote: "Their collapse in the 1340s has been attributed by most historians to huge losses on loans to Edward III of England. This article explores some flaws in the conventional explanation and shows that the firms lacked the resources to have made loans on the generally accepted scale." So the standard story is contested; the lender was repaid-or-not by a sovereign, not underpriced in a market.

18. Medici Bank (1494). Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medici_Bank (secondary). It states that the London branch "got into trouble for much the same reason the Bruges branch would, unwisely loaning large sums to secular rulers, a group notorious for their delinquencies", and that the Lyons branch manager "had attempted to cover up his incompetence by being far too optimistic as to the number of bad loans the branch would have to cover". Cambridge chapter on the London branch (https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/progress-and-problems-in-medieval-england/lorenzo-de-medicis-london-branch/2E4EDA1AB98762E9165D05A4175181D9): de Roover "attributed much importance to independent loans to King Edward IV parallel to the Bruges branch's loans to Charles the Bold", and "his account is not final".

19. Rome, AD 33. Source: Tacitus, Annals 6.16-17 (Wikisource link above). Quotes: "Hence followed a scarcity of money, a great shock being given to all credit"; "Creditors however were suing for payment in full ... the usurers had hoarded up all their money for buying land. The facilities for selling were followed by a fall of prices ... many were utterly ruined." Relief: "the emperor interposed his aid by distributing throughout the banks a hundred million sesterces, and allowing freedom to borrow without interest for three years, provided the borrower gave security to the State in land to double the amount." Note this is a credit squeeze after a law was invoked, not a case of banks lending at too low a rate.

20. Roman argentarii failures. No source found. Searches returned only popular pages (snippet only: the phrase "foro cedere" for going bankrupt). Andreau's Cambridge excerpt and the Library of Congress sample (https://assets.cambridge.org/97805213/89327/excerpt/9780521389327_excerpt.pdf, https://catdir.loc.gov/catdir/samples/cam032/98048325.pdf) contain no passage on bank failures. The Bryn Mawr Classical Review notice (https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/?p=21930) says only that "only professional bankers (never the elite financiers) were subject" to certain regulation; I did not see what follows. Treat Roman banker failure rates and causes as unmeasured.

Pattern the sources do support: losses came from lending to sovereigns or from credit squeezes and from bad-loan concealment, not from a documented case of a competitive lender cutting its rate below its cost.

## 4. Household wealth relative to income, and its forms

21. National wealth was about seven years of national income in 1700, mostly land.
    Source: https://zenodo.org/records/3458611 (Scheidel, Roman Wealth and Wealth Inequality in Comparative Perspective, preprint 2019, PDF).
    Quote: "In France in 1700, total national wealth is estimated to have equaled seven times annual national income, a rough equivalent of GDP. Almost two-thirds of that wealth was embodied in agricultural land, with the remainder fairly evenly split between housing stock and other domestic capital. In Britain in 1700, by contrast, the wealth/income ratio was the same (7 to 1) but, due to greater development, farmland played a somewhat smaller role and domestic capital a larger one."
    Corroboration: Piketty and Zucman slides, https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/PikettyZucman2014Slides.pdf, slide title "In 18c Old World, land/Y as high as 400%", with UK Figure 3 (read from the image) showing total near 700 percent of national income in 1700 and agricultural land near 400 percent.
    Scheidel notes "all these breakdowns must be taken with a healthy dose of caution". These are national, not household, figures and 1700, not 1300 or Rome.

22. Roman breakdown by analogy (Scheidel, same PDF): "if total Roman assets were worth around 140 billion sesterces, agricultural land would have been worth c.90 billion, housing c.20 billion, other domestic capital another c.20 billion, and slaves c.10 billion", against a GDP he sets at about 18 to 20 billion (n.12 "18 billion"). This is a model by analogy, not an observation. Goldsmith's earlier figure valued land at "17 times annual yields of 6 percent", i.e. a 6 percent rent/price assumed, not measured.

23. Coin was a small share of wealth. King's breakdown, England late 17th century: coinage "less than 4 percent of all wealth" (Scheidel, same PDF). Duncan-Jones's estimate for Rome would make coin "about one-seventh of total wealth", which Scheidel judges "likely to be inflated".

24. Net saving was very low. Scheidel, using Piketty's wealth-to-income relation: "If we put β at 7-8 and g at 0.2-0.25, s would be a little over 1½ percent of GDP ... 4 or 5 sesterces per person per year, a minuscule amount." This is a derived figure, resting on an assumed growth rate. It implies that the flow of new savings into lending was small relative to the stock of wealth, and that most of the stock sat in land, housing, tools and slaves.

25. Household-level probate wealth by income multiple: no source found. The core.ac.uk page (https://core.ac.uk/works/5428273) returned a bot challenge, so the first pass's probate figures are unverified.

26. Top wealth shares (relevant to who lends): "top 1 percent wealth shares in several European countries ranged from about one-third to a little more than half of national wealth: c.35 percent in Finland, c.45 percent in France and Norway, c.50 percent in Sweden, and c.55 percent in Denmark and the United Kingdom" around 1770-1810 (Scheidel PDF). A thin top held most of the lendable wealth.
