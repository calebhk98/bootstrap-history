# Research: how an innovator's price path should emerge (Complaint 336)

Owner decision on Complaint 336: a new technique's output starts near the incumbent price and falls as its maker competes for customers, as an outcome of profit-seeking against demand and rivals, never a hardcoded rule. If a rule is needed the model is wrong somewhere. The payback floor becomes a diagnostic, never a cap.

Tags: **[read]** the file or paper text was read in this session; **[snippet]** only a search summary was seen; **[recalled]** the author's own recollection of the literature, not verified. Code references are to the branch `structural-dedupe-and-owner-decisions` and were all read.

## 1. How prices form today, and why the path does or does not emerge

There are two price layers, and the complaint touches both.

### 1.1 The agent economy (the default economy)

- **Clearing.** `sim/economy/goods_market.py` clears each (good, area) once a year at a single uniform price: the buyers' schedules (a floor plus a part that falls with price, capped by budget, `quantity_at`) meet a step supply built from sellers' reservation prices. `solve_price` (`goods_market_solve.py`) finds the crossing. Lowest reservations sell first, the marginal group pro rata, and every seller is paid the clearing price. This is a call auction.
- **Sellers are price-takers.** `producers.offers` offers all held output at one reservation: the expected price less carrying cost and spoilage (`inventory.holding_reservation`), lowered when cash is short. The unit cost does not enter the offer. The producer's cost enters only in the year's run decision (`plan`: run while expected revenue covers variable cost) and in `next_expectations`.
- **Expected price is not chosen, it is adapted.** `next_expectations` moves the expected output price part of the way to last year's clearing price, then part of the way toward the break-even price (`REGRESSIVE_EXPECTATION_WEIGHT`, a Nerlove-style expectation), and marks it down after an unsold year. No producer asks "what price maximises my profit given the demand I face and my rivals' supply?".
- **Price memory** (`market_memory*.py`) moves a remembered price by trade volume relative to the market's usual volume, so a sliver of trade does not reset it.
- **Capacity growth.** Producers with plant ask for a loan to grow by a bounded share of capacity a year when the return on a new run beats the interest rate (`producers_close._expansion`, `EXPANSION_SHARE_PER_YEAR`); output follows last year's expected price within a bounded yearly change (`within_a_years_change`, `OUTPUT_CHANGE_SHARE_PER_YEAR`); workplaces inside one producer have a spread of costs so output rises smoothly with price (`COST_SPREAD_WITHIN_PRODUCER`, `share_working`).
- **Entry.** `entry.entry_plans` starts a new maker only where buyers wanted more than was sold, beyond idle capacity, and only for a share of that gap, held to a share of last year's traded volume (`entry_sizing.py`). A market with buyers and no maker draws a trial newcomer judged at its own full cost plus a margin (`entry_trial.py`). Entry drawn by a lasting margin over cost where makers exist was tried and removed because it destabilised staple prices (`Complaints/reports/agent-economy-review-round-four.md`, "Tried and not merged"; `docs/architecture/ECONOMY_AGENTS.md`, "Demand brings makers").
- **Exit.** A producer whose year did not cover costs and plant charge counts a loss year; after enough of them, with no variable margin, it exits and its lenders are repaid first (`producers_close.py`, `producer_exit.py`).

What follows for a near-zero-cost technique in a market that already clears:

1. A small entrant earns the full gap between the clearing price and its cost. That is correct behaviour, and it is the owner's "start near the market price": a price-taker among many sellers starts at the incumbent price without any rule. The agent economy already produces the first half of the path.
2. The entrant does not grow, because a market that clears has no unmet demand, so `entry_plans` plans no maker, and margin-driven entry was removed. A cheap technique already known to the society therefore has no route into a cleared market except through producers already running the recipe. So the price does not fall, the incumbents do not exit, and the margin persists as a rent with no mechanism eroding it. This is the specific reason the second half of the path (cut as capacity grows, incumbents leave) does not emerge.
3. Where an entrant does hold capacity, nothing makes it use its position. A seller with a large share of the market and a cost far below its rivals' still offers everything at its expected price, so it neither undercuts to take customers nor restrains output to hold price. Only the volume-weighted memory and the regressive expectation move its ask.

### 1.2 The engine's yearly market for derived concerns (the founder's firms)

- `sim/engine/incumbent_prices.py` sets a cost reference per good: the incumbents' solved cost, in labour hours, under the techniques the society started with (`granted`), with a labelled fallback to the cheapest route among techniques now run for a good no incumbent makes.
- `sim/world/market.py` clears a posted ratio of spot price over that reference (`clearing_price_ratio`), held between a floor ratio and a ceiling ratio (`DEFAULT_FLOOR_RATIO`, `DEFAULT_CEILING_RATIO`, both temporary heuristics). Supply is the society's capacity (with a short-run elasticity), actors' output, offers with reservations, the founder's sales and carried stock.
- A producer's offer is its own entry's unit cost over the incumbents' cost (`producer_costs.py`, `founder_concern_offers`): reservation, not price. The founder's concern sells at the posted price, uniform for every seller.
- `concern_volume.py` computes the concern's volume and value ratios at the incumbents' prices; takings are the volume times the market's spot-over-incumbent ratio.
- Capacity follows price: `adjusted_capacity` raises society capacity while the price ratio exceeds one and cuts it below, by a bounded step. Incumbents idle at the floor; they do not exit on cost.

Consequences for the complaint: the concern's margin is the posted price (anchored to the dearer incumbent route) minus its own cost, a monopoly margin when its volume is small, which is the complaint's first fault. The posted price does fall as the founder's tonnes add supply, so the shape of the path exists, but (a) the floor ratio is a hardcoded stand-in for the incumbents' variable cost, so for a drastic innovation the price stops at an authored number, not at a demand-determined one; (b) incumbent capacity shrinks by a rate rule on the price gap, not by their own losses; (c) the founder's concern capacity is fixed by its staff and plant, with no decision about how much to build against the demand it faces; (d) the reference `incumbent_prices` is a cost lookup that the market ratio multiplies, so the "price" is cost-derived by construction, against CLAUDE.md 4.5 in spirit. The long-run solver (`solve_prices*.py`) is the legitimate long-run cost reference (zero-profit entry price) and must stay a diagnostic and a fallback, not the posted price.

### 1.3 The payback floor today

`test_early_playtest.py` ("no node repays its entire cost in under three months", `PUMP_PAYBACK_YEARS_FLOOR`) tests build cost over gross revenue (`rev`). Gross revenue counts wages passed to staff, so a labour-heavy node sits near the floor while netting nothing (the complaint's second fault). A cap on revenue would also contradict the owner's decision: a new technique may earn a fortune until its own output drives the price down.

## 2. The mechanism a profit-seeking seller needs

### 2.1 What the literature says

- **Drastic versus non-drastic innovation** (Arrow 1962) [snippet: the search summary of secondary sources, https://www.minneapolisfed.org/research/sr/sr402.pdf and https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/108121/1/MPRA_paper_108121.pdf]. A process innovation that lowers constant marginal cost is drastic when the monopoly price at the new cost is below the old cost: the innovator then prices as an unconstrained monopolist and the incumbents are out. When it is non-drastic the innovator is constrained by the old technology: the limit price is just under the incumbents' cost, and the innovator sells all it can at it. Both cases fall out of one profit maximisation against the residual demand left after rivals' supply; the distinction is not a rule to author but a property of the demand, the costs and the capacity. [recalled for the limit-price detail]
- **Dominant firm with a competitive fringe** (Forchheimer; Stigler; Gaskins 1971 for the dynamic version) [recalled]. The dominant firm faces residual demand equal to market demand minus the fringe's supply at each price, and picks the price maximising price less its marginal cost times the smaller of its capacity and the residual quantity. The fringe supplies more at higher prices, so the dominant firm trades price against fringe growth; in the dynamic version it limits price to slow fringe entry. This is the closest textbook shape to the owner's coffee example, and it needs only what a seller can observe.
- **Bertrand-Edgeworth competition with capacity limits** [snippet: https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/7946/1/MPRA_paper_7946.pdf, https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/bitstreams/55067833-91dd-439d-ae73-ac8f6ace7d1d/download]. With homogeneous goods and binding capacities, pure-price equilibrium may not exist; mixed strategies or price cycles (Edgeworth cycles) arise. A yearly posted-price market with capacity-limited sellers inherits this instability. A seller with capacity below demand can sell all of it at a price above the rival's, because the rival cannot serve everyone, so capacity limits create a high-price region where undercutting is not profitable.
- **Capacity commitment then price competition** (Kreps and Scheinkman 1983) [snippet: same links; abstract summary]: capacity chosen first, price second, gives the Cournot outcome under efficient rationing. It is the right reading of "capacity growth over time" in a yearly step: the year's price is a clearing, the strategic choice is how much capacity to commit. Under other rationing rules the result can fail [snippet].
- **Limit pricing as signalling** (Milgrom and Roberts 1982) [recalled]: needs private cost information; not needed here since costs are observable in the economy's own books.
- **Switching costs and penetration pricing** (Klemperer 1987) [snippet: https://ideas.repec.org/a/rje/randje/v18y1987ispringp138-150.html, https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/DP704.html]. With consumer switching costs a firm invests in market share by pricing low early and harvests later ("bargain then rip-off"). It is the opposite sign to the owner's coffee example, which starts high with no switching costs. The two are compatible: without switching costs a seller starts at the incumbent price because it can sell its whole small capacity there; with switching costs and the prospect of a large share, it prices low early. Both come from one profit calculation, with the buyers' side deciding.
- **Industry dynamics with entry and exit** (Hopenhayn 1992; Ericson and Pakes 1995) [snippet: https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1992/09/01/entry-exit-and-firm-dynamics-long-run-equilibrium, https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v62y1995i1p53-82..html]. Firms enter when expected value beats entry cost and exit when value falls below scrap; in a stationary state the price equals the marginal entrant's cost. They are the reference for the long-run price the solver computes, and for exit at a loss.
- **Posted prices with adaptive markups in agent-based macro.**
  - Keynes+Schumpeter (Dosi, Fagiolo, Roventini) [read: the 2012 working paper reusing the model, https://www.ofce.fr/pdf/dtravail/WP2012-06.pdf, section 3.3]: a consumption-good firm sets price as a markup over unit cost; the markup is raised when the firm's market share grew last period and cut when it shrank, `markup(t) = markup(t-1) * (1 + speed * (share(t-1) - share(t-2)) / share(t-2))`. Market shares follow a replicator dynamic on competitiveness, which falls in price and in unfilled demand, so even with a homogeneous good firms charge different prices. Capacity (investment) is expansion plus replacement with a delivery lag; firms with near-zero shares exit and are replaced. A low-cost firm in this model starts with the economy's markup, gains share, raises the markup while share grows, and its price falls only because rivals' prices and its own unfilled demand move shares. Prices fall with unit cost only in proportion to the markup rule.
  - Lengnick baseline [read: https://legacy.econ.tuwien.ac.at/lva/compeco.se/artikel/jebo_2013_agent_based_macroeconomics_a_baseline_model.pdf]: a firm considers a price change only with unsatisfying inventory (raise on low stock, cut on high), within bounds set relative to marginal cost, by a small random step, and only with some probability; households search and rewire to cheaper sellers with a probability and a threshold. No entry or exit. Local knowledge only: firms do not know competitors' prices.
  - Delli Gatti, Gallegati et al. (CATS) and EURACE [snippet only: https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0312096, https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-00339756]: not read in detail; the rule details were not retrieved. `Complaints/reports/economy-research-price-stability-and-entry.md` (an earlier research brief in the repo, read) records that these models signal on inventory against demand and that none uses margin-driven entry.
  - Common to these: price is a seller's decision adapted by feedback from share or inventory, not an optimum against a known demand; the speed parameters are heuristics. They show an innovator's margin shrinking as its share rises, because the feedback raises the firm's price only while its share grows, and share growth pushes rivals out.
- **Walrasian or call-auction clearing versus posted prices** [recalled, with supply-function equilibrium for uniform-price auctions: Klemperer and Meyer 1989, snippet at https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/7260 and https://cepr.org/publications/dp4363]. Under uniform-price clearing every seller is paid the crossing; a seller with a small share is a price-taker and earns the whole gap between the crossing and its cost, a seller with a large share moves the crossing by its own sales and so restrains or undercuts; with strategic supply functions there is a continuum of equilibria (collusive-looking ones included), so a simple rule for the supply function is needed. Under posted prices the seller picks its price, and with capacity limits the Edgeworth instability above appears; the innovator's margin then depends on the buyer-search rule (Lengnick) or the share rule (K+S), both heuristics.

### 2.2 What the seller knows, chooses and learns

Recommended for this simulator (the yearly call auction stays; the seller's reservation becomes a decision):

- **Knows.** Its own unit cost (it runs the costing already), its capacity built and coming (`record.expansion_runs`), its stock, and last year's market book: buyers' schedules and sellers' reservations, which `market_curves.summarize` already stores as plain rows. From the book it derives its residual demand: the buyers' schedule at each price minus the supply rivals offered at or below that price. Rivals' costs are never read directly; only their offered reservations, as in a real market where asks are visible. Where the book is stale (a market that did not trade, `market_memory`), the seller falls back to the current rule.
- **Chooses.** A reservation price for its offered quantity (this year, within the existing yearly change limit), by maximising `(price - own_unit_cost) * min(capacity_in_stock, residual_demand_at(price))` over the observed residual demand. Because lower reservations sell first and everyone is paid the crossing, a high reservation withholds output (restraint) and a low one only matters if rivals' reservations sit below it. This is the dominant-firm-with-fringe problem solved on the book, one-dimensional, deterministic given the book, and needs no authored path.
- **Capacity.** Keep the existing loan-financed expansion rule (return above the live rate). Add that the expected return is computed at the price the seller expects after its own added capacity, not at last year's price: this is what limits capacity growth by demand (Kreps and Scheinkman) and counts capacity under construction as supply. The research brief in the repo already lists "an anticipated own-price impact" as damping that acts on what planners know.
- **Learns.** If the book is unavailable, estimate residual elasticity from the last two years' (price, quantity) pairs, or move the reservation by the share-feedback rule of K+S (markup up while share grows). Label the speed as a temporary heuristic.
- **Switching costs.** Do not add them in the first stages. The call auction treats buyers as anonymous and homogeneous, so the coffee path is produced without them. A later stage can add them as buyer inertia: a buyer keeps last year's seller unless the saving exceeds a switching cost drawn from the buyer's own situation (retooling, relationships); with them a large entrant would price low early (Klemperer). That requires seller-specific bids, which `Bid` lacks.
- **Incumbent exit.** Leave as is: a producer exits through repeated loss years, lenders first. The entrant's lower price pushes incumbents' reservations above the crossing, so they sell nothing, the variable-margin test idles them, and the loss-year count removes them. No exit rule is added, and the engine layer's price floor ratio becomes the incumbents' own variable cost, derived, instead of an authored ratio.
- **Information.** One seller sees the book of its own market area only; it does not see other areas.

### 2.3 Which model fits a yearly step

The dominant firm with a competitive fringe (static each year), with capacity committed in the previous stage (Kreps and Scheinkman), fits best: the yearly price is already a clearing, the seller's strategic variable is its reservation and capacity, and the time dimension comes from capacity growth, stock and the fringe's exit. Bertrand-Edgeworth posted prices would reintroduce mixed-strategy noise in a one-tick-a-year model (the design choice already made in `ECONOMY_AGENTS.md`, "Annual step, clearing within the year"). The K+S markup rule is the fallback heuristic for goods whose book is not observable, not the main mechanism.

### 2.4 How the path then comes about

- Year one, small capacity: residual demand is almost the whole market less the fringe's supply at each price. The best reservation is anywhere at or below the incumbents' price, and the entrant sells all its output at the crossing, which is the incumbent price. The margin is large and bounded by demand.
- As capacity grows, the entrant's output moves the crossing. Its optimal reservation stops being "sell everything" and becomes the point where marginal revenue on the residual demand equals its cost, or the capacity limit, whichever binds. If its monopoly price on the whole demand is below the incumbents' variable cost (drastic), incumbents sell nothing and the entrant is the market; otherwise the price settles just under the dearest incumbent that is still needed (limit price) and the incumbents at the margin leave.
- The entrant stops expanding when the return at the price it expects after its own capacity falls to the interest rate, so profit stays bounded by what demand supports. The long-run price approaches the entrant's full cost (the solver's long-run reference) only if rivals with the same technique enter; the first entrant keeps a rent until they do, exactly like the automated loom.
- Imitators enter through the existing path (a known recipe, a gap, a loan), now made possible because the cleared market has unmet demand at the low price: the entrant's undercutting raises quantity demanded beyond what the entrant sells, which `unmet_quantity` already measures. That closes the loop without bringing back margin-based entry.

## 3. What the payback floor should become

The test stops capping revenue and becomes a diagnostic with a precise definition and a clear exemption for a legitimate fortune:

- **Measure net, not gross.** Compare build cost with net takings: sales of outputs less purchases, wages and derived upkeep (the complaint's second fault; `concern_volume._net_value` already works net of purchases; wages come from the staff the concern holds).
- **What a fault looks like (flag, never cap):**
  1. *Price never falls despite rising supply.* The concern's output (or the good's total traded volume) rises over successive years while the market price of the good stays flat or rises, with no change in demand drivers (population, income, money). In a working market the price falls along residual demand; flat means the seller is not on a demand curve (the current engine and agent-economy gap above).
  2. *Revenue above what demand allows.* Yearly revenue of the concern exceeds the maximum over price of price times market demand at that price, `max_price(price * demand(price))`, within the market area; that is a money pump, whatever the payback.
  3. *Rent without growth or imitation.* A margin well above the interest-rate return persists for many years with no capacity growth by the owner and no entry, where the loan path was open: a stuck expansion or entry rule.
  4. *Payback faster than the build time of the capacity that earns it.* Distinct from a high return: a build that repays before it can exist indicates a cost the model omitted (plant, working capital, training). Compare against the gestation lag, not a fixed floor.
  5. *Margin that does not shrink as the entrant's share rises.* The entrant's price-cost gap should fall (or its share growth stall) as its share of the market rises; a gap that stays while share climbs means incumbents keep selling at a price they cannot survive, or demand is unbounded.
- **What is not a fault.** A very short payback with a small share of a large market (the coffee entrant's first year), a labour-heavy node whose gross revenue is mostly wages (judged net), or a fortune that falls over the following years.
- **Where it lives.** A `simulator.py` subcommand or a slow test in the economy test package that runs the scenario and reports the flagged pattern, with the pass condition stated qualitatively: the flagged pattern absent over an ensemble of seeds, never a dated outcome (CLAUDE.md 4.2). No numbers go into prose; the threshold constants carry `declare(...)` labels.

## 4. What must not be authored, build plan, tests, open questions

### 4.1 Must not be authored (CLAUDE.md 4.1, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5, 4.7)

- A price path, a decay curve, a "start at the incumbent price then drop" schedule, or an innovator flag or counter.
- A markup constant by good or a minimum margin, a cap on revenue or profit, or the payback floor as a limit.
- A limit price per good or a reference to a node, recipe or civilisation id in the seller's decision.
- A floor ratio or ceiling ratio standing in for incumbents' variable cost (the existing ones in `sim/world/market.py` are already tagged temporary heuristics and must be retired, not extended).
- The incumbents' exit date, share or number.
- The entrant's capacity schedule.
- A price table; the long-run solver may serve as a fallback and a diagnostic, not as a posted price (4.5).
- Anything specific to the founder: the seller's decision is written against an actor, so a firm, a state or another player uses it unchanged (CLAUDE.md section 5, "General actors").

Allowed and labelled as temporary heuristics: adaptation speeds (the share-feedback speed, the yearly change in reservation), the number of years of book memory, the share of capacity added per year (existing).

### 4.2 Staged plan (each stage opens with a failing regression test, then the change)

**Stage 0. Measure the present behaviour (tests only).** In the economy test package (alongside `test_economy_entry.py` and `test_economy_entry_trial.py`, using `economy_fixture.py`), add a coffee-style scenario: a market with several incumbent makers on a dear technique that clear a known demand; a second recipe for the same good at a far lower cost becomes known. Record, year by year: the clearing price, the volume, the entrant's capacity, margin, and the incumbents' capacity and exits. Assert what is true today and mark as expected failures the four target properties below. This pins fault 2 above (no growth in a cleared market) with a smallest reproducing scenario, as CLAUDE.md section 6 requires. Also add a test of the engine layer: the posted ratio for a drastic innovator stops at the floor ratio.

**Stage 1. Observable residual demand.** Expose from the market book (`market_curves.summarize`, kept per market after the clearing) a pure function: residual demand for a seller at a price given the book and the seller's own offer removed. Unit-test it against hand-computed schedules (a single buyer schedule and a few rivals), including the stale-book and no-offer cases. No behaviour change.

**Stage 2. A seller chooses its reservation.** Replace the expected-price-based reservation of a producer whose share of the market is material by the maximiser of `(price - own cost) * min(capacity, residual demand)`; small producers keep the price-taking rule (they converge to it anyway since their effect on the crossing is nil). Behaviour tests:
  - *Starts near the incumbent price.* In year one a small entrant's sales price is within a small tolerance of the incumbents' price (the margin is large).
  - *Bounded by demand.* The entrant's revenue never exceeds the maximum revenue demand allows at any price in any year (the diagnostic 2 above applied as an assertion).
  - *Falls as capacity grows.* Across an ensemble of seeds, the price in the last years of the scenario is lower than in the first, and the price series is non-increasing in the large (allow noise) while the entrant's capacity rises.
  - *Drastic versus non-drastic.* Two scenarios differing only in the entrant's cost: with a cost far below the incumbents' the incumbents sell nothing within a few years; with a cost only a little below, the price settles just below the incumbents' reservation and the incumbents keep part of the market. Assert the ordering, not magnitudes.
  - *Fingerprint.* Run `python3 -m sim.tests.fingerprint` before and after; the behaviour of markets without a dominant seller must not change.

**Stage 3. Capacity against expected own impact.** Compute the return used by `_expansion` at the price expected after the producer's own planned additions, and count `expansion_runs` as coming supply. Tests: capacity growth stops where the return at the post-expansion price equals the interest rate; the entrant's profit falls with its share; incumbents with a higher cost exit through the existing loss-year path (assert the exit and the lender repayment).

**Stage 4. Imitation closes the loop.** Check, with a test, that the undercutting raises unmet demand at the lower price, so `entry_plans` brings further makers of the cheap recipe, and that the price then approaches the cheap recipe's full cost over the long run while the first entrant's margin shrinks.

**Stage 5. Engine layer and the diagnostic.** Retire the posted-ratio floor in `sim/world/market.py` in favour of the incumbents' own variable cost for the concern's good; route the founder's concern through the same seller decision (the engine's `founder_concern_offers`). Re-define the payback test (section 3) as the diagnostic, net of upkeep. The `Complaints/336` status moves to closed when the two original faults have regression tests.

**Stage 6 (optional, later). Switching costs.** Add seller-specific buyer inertia with a cost drawn from buyers' own state; test that with switching costs a large entrant prices lower in its first years than without (Klemperer's penetration), and that without them the coffee path of Stage 2 holds.

### 4.3 Open questions

1. Do rivals' asks need to be visible, or only the clearing price and volume? The first needs the book kept per market (`market_curves` already summarises it); the second means estimating elasticity, which is noisier and slower. The choice changes how fast the path emerges.
2. Do we want the seller to withhold output (a reservation above the crossing leaves goods unsold and carried as stock)? It is what a profit-maximising dominant firm does, but it also makes shortages in staples. Staples might need the entrant to be capacity-limited by cash or by a demand floor first (`Complaints/reports/agent-economy-review-round-four.md` shows volatility sensitivity), so Stage 2 should be run on non-staples first and measured on the grain volatility checks.
3. How is "material share" defined without a magic number? One option: a seller acts strategically exactly when its own removal moves the crossing; that is derived from the book, not a threshold.
4. Strategic supply functions have a continuum of equilibria in uniform-price auctions [snippet]; a single reservation per seller is a restriction, and a choice of equilibrium (the one the profit calculation picks) must be stated as a heuristic.
5. Two layers of price (engine posted ratio against the agent economy) must converge before the founder's concern is rewritten; the Stage 5 order depends on how far the agent economy's port has replaced the engine market (`ECONOMY_AGENTS.md`, "The founder's concerns sell in the same markets"; the takings return through `edge:legacy`, Complaint 382).
6. Buyers' side: in the call auction buyers are anonymous, so lock-in and brand loyalty have no place; is the Klemperer extension worth the `Bid` change?
7. Land-bound and deposit-bound recipes: the entrant's cost includes rent derived from the same market, so a cheap technique that needs scarce land earns rent not profit; the diagnostic must not flag it. The tests need a scenario with a site limit.
8. Delli Gatti et al. and EURACE rules were not read in full; if the posted-price fallback (K+S rule) is adopted, read their pricing equations before choosing.
