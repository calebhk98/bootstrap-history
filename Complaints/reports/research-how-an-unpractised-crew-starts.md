## Real-world practice (sources opened)

Opened in this session: Wallis (LSE WP 22/07, PDF, converted with pdftotext), Epstein 1998 (PDF, pdftotext; OCR is run-together, quotes below are restored spacing), Arrow 1962 and Spence 1981 abstract pages (RePEc/EconPapers), Melitz 2005 abstract page (RePEc), Acemoglu-Pischke NBER w6357 page (excerpt only). Not opened: Becker 1964 itself, Spence's full text, Humphries, Minns full text, Campbell. The two earlier repository reports (`research-how-a-brand-new-trade-forms.md`, `research-how-trades-spread-and-skill-premia.md`) contain further quotes I re-did not open this session, so I do not rely on them below except as pointers.

1. **Apprenticeship: the learner's output pays for most of the learning; who pays the rest is a premium or low wage.**
   - Wallis, "Apprenticeship and training in premodern England" (LSE Economic History WP 22/07, 2007), https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/Research/FACTS/WorkingPapers/2007/2207Wallis.pdf, p. 23-24: apprentices "provided valuable labour services throughout their term, rather than repaying their masters' investment just in the later years of service"; "the costs of supplying this training were low enough that they could be met by the value of less-skilled apprentices' work (at times in combination with a premium), even after the costs of their keep"; "Premodern apprenticeship was not a two-stage process of costly training followed by repayment, but a time when work and training were often indistinguishable."
   - Same paper, p. 18: "Clearly, apprentices had good reason to invest in their training; masters much less so." and premiums are "the price of access to the best masters"; "masters demanded significant inducements before agreeing to take apprentices on." And it denies the textbook assumption that training costs are "high relative to the value of a new apprentices' labour, and ... must therefore be born, at least initially, by masters" (p. 18, calls it "suspect"). Reading: the learner (or family) bore the cost, through a premium and unpaid or low-paid work; the master bore little because the learner's work was not a loss.
   - Epstein, "Craft Guilds, Apprenticeship, and Technological Change in Preindustrial Europe", J. Economic History 58(3), 1998, https://bollettinoadapt.it/wp-content/uploads/epstein1998.pdf, p. 688-690: the question "is not whether training in skills was costless or unnecessary (it was neither), but ... how to allocate costs to provide teachers and pupils with adequate incentives"; masters "raised the trainee's cost of default by demanding entry fees, by setting apprentices' wages on a rising scale for the contract's duration, and by promising a pay-off upon completion." Reading: a rising-wage contract is the cost of learning carried forward and repaid from the practised years: the same structure as an investment.
   - Becker (general versus specific training): not opened. A web search summary (secondary, not Becker's text) says general training is paid by the worker through lower wages, specific training shared. Acemoglu-Pischke, NBER w6357 (opened, excerpt only): frictions "compress the structure of wages in the general skills of their employees", so firms may sponsor general training. Treat Becker as "secondary only".

2. **Skill and new technique moving by people, not by waiting to be learned.** Epstein 1998, p. 702 (same PDF): "In preindustrial economies, technological cross-fertilization occurred overwhelmingly through artisan migration. Technological transfer took place through the permanent emigration of master artisans and the temporary migration of journeymen." Migration was a state-backed transfer of cost: rulers competed for craftsmen (earlier report, not re-opened: a Murano glassmaker paid five to ten times his Venetian earnings). The pattern at line level that I did open: the importing side paid the premium to avoid the learning years.

3. **Learning by doing as an investment with a forward-looking price.**
   - Arrow, "The Economic Implications of Learning by Doing", Review of Economic Studies 29(3), 1962, 155-173, https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v29y1962i3p155-173..html (abstract only): knowledge grows with experience, and cannot be treated as exogenous growth in time. The full model (learning a function of cumulative investment) I did not open.
   - Spence, "The Learning Curve and Competition", Bell Journal of Economics 12(1), 1981, 49-70, https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:rje:bellje:v:12:y:1981:i:spring:p:49-70 (abstract only): "This article develops a model of competitive interaction and industry evolution in the presence of a learning curve" and the learning curve is unit cost as a function of the firm's accumulated volume. That a forward-looking firm prices or produces below current cost to move down the curve is the standard reading of Spence but I did not open text that says so: "no source found" in this session for the below-cost statement. A search summary of Fudenberg-Tirole called it incentives that "encourage the incumbent to overaccumulate" (secondary, not opened).
   - Melitz, "When and How Should Infant Industries Be Protected?", J. International Economics 66, 2005, 177-196, https://ideas.repec.org/p/hrv/faseco/3228378.html (abstract only): the learning is a spillover external to firms; "protection isn't always optimal even with a learning externality; it depends on the industry's learning potential and the shape of its learning curve". Reading for the code: when the learning is internal to the producer (its own crew practises on its own runs, as here), no subsidy argument arises: the producer should itself weigh the investment. Only the part that spills to peers (the entrant taking the peers' mean practice) is an externality, and the code already models that spillover in the right direction (peers' practice lowers an entrant's start).
   - Humphries, Minns: no source opened in this session; no source found.

4. **Customers and prices.** I found no source I opened that says novices sold at lower prices. The repository already spoils part of a novice run's output (`SCRAPPED_SHARE_OF_EXTRA_EFFORT`, with its own opened sources), which is the quality channel.

Summary of the real-world pattern, limited to what was opened: the learner or family pays (premium, low or no wage) while the learner's work covers most of the keep; the cost is repaid from the practised years (rising wage scale); firms and states pay to import skill instead of waiting; nobody in the sources just refuses to produce until trained. The investment is judged against the practised stream.

## Recommended rule

Follow the real world as (a), made an explicit investment judged against the practised stream, and share it between the plan and the entry decision. Not (b) alone (it is already coded and does not help a trade nobody practises) and not (c) (capacity that cannot be staffed is not the problem; the staffed crew refuses to run).

**Rule: a producer plans on the level cost of its learning path.** Instead of judging a run at this year's hours and yield, the plan judges the *level* (time-averaged, discounted) revenue and variable cost of the run over the years it expects to keep the trade, in which the crew climbs the learning curve and ends at the floor. The sum of discounted profit over that path being negative is exactly "give up": `share_working` of the level pair is small. The extra hours of early years are therefore weighed against the margin of the practised years, in one smooth number.

**Which functions change**
1. New plain function in `sim/economy/practice.py` (or a new small file `practice_path.py` beside it, per the short-file rule), `learning_path_scales(units_by_trade, runs_per_workshop_per_year, horizon_years, discount_rate)`, returning for each trade the level hours multiplier and for the recipe the level usable-output share. Computation in words: for each year in the horizon, starting at the crew's present cumulative runs per trade, take the year's hours multiplier from `hours_multiplier(units)` and the year's usable share from `usable_output_share` of booked and practised hours; weight the year by one over (one plus the discount rate) to the power of the years elapsed; the level multiplier is the weighted sum of multipliers divided by the sum of weights; the level usable share is likewise; between years the units advance by `runs_per_workshop_per_year` as `crew_runs_after_year` does (hired share zero). After the floor is reached the multiplier is one and the share one.
2. `producers.plan` (producers.py:306-329) and the kernel `plan_rows` (plan_kernel.py:100-190), at the single place where the share is chosen (producers.py:328, plan_kernel.py:160), use a **level pair**: `level_revenue = revenue_per_run at full yield * level usable share * yield factor`, `level_variable = input cost + rent + labour cost computed at the level hours multipliers`. The decision is `capacity * share_working(level_revenue, level_variable, spread)`. Everything that pays or bids stays on the **current** year's practised hours and yield: `hours_out`, wage line, input bids, `affordable` (cash binds at the actual cost), the ceiling `room`. The kernel gets one more per-row array like `hours_scale` (`level_hours_scale`) plus a per-row level yield factor; the loop's second `hours_bill` call is the only added work and only for rows still learning (rows with empty `crew_runs` skip it, so established producers stay bit-identical, as the fingerprint requires for them).
3. `entry_practice.planned_recipe` (entry_practice.py:101-121) and `entry_tables` first hours (entry_tables.py:99): the entry return uses the same `learning_path_scales` with the same horizon and rate in place of the mean over `LOSS_YEARS_BEFORE_EXIT`, so entry and the new producer's plan agree. The stake for a newcomer (entry_year.py:155-156) stays at current first-year hours, but is additionally bounded by financing below.

**Inputs of the rule (all existing except two):** the producer's cumulative runs by trade (`Producer.crew_runs`), the pace `runs_per_workshop_per_year` = `capacity_runs / workshops` (the runs it would work if the trade paid; this keeps the rule free of the circularity with the runs being chosen), the market interest rate of the producer's currency (`view.interest_rate`, already used at entry.py:152), the booked recipe, the two constants below.

**New declared constants (2)**
- `YEARS_A_WORKSHOP_EXPECTS_TO_KEEP_A_TRADE`: `kind="temporary_heuristic"`, `source=None`, `confidence="D"`, unit years; retire = the owner's working life from the cohort age distribution and the plant's `plant_life_years` where the recipe has plant (`Complaints/644`, per-worker skill in the labour market). Real-world pointer only: Wallis p. 23-24 puts apprenticeship terms of several years and the learner's work repaying the keep within the term; I opened no figure for how long a master expected to keep a trade, so no number is sourced. Cap by the recipe's plant life where it has plant (a rule, not a number).
- `LEARNING_FINANCED_SHARE_OF_STAKE` is **not** added; use the existing stake and loan (below). Discount rate is the market rate, no new constant.

**Financing (no new constant).** The learning years' losses must be financed out of the stake and the loan: bound the planned runs so that the largest cumulative shortfall of the path per run times runs does not exceed `cash + held value + loan headroom`; in the kernel this is the existing `affordable` term extended to the first-year cost, which it already is (`paid_now` is current-hours), so a plan on level costs cannot exceed the cash that pays the real, higher bill. If cash is short the producer runs fewer runs and learns more slowly, which `crew_runs_after_year` already reflects: continuous, not a stop. The owner's top-up `restake` (entry.py:88-93) pays only when `pays` is true (judged at current hours) and should use the level pair too, otherwise the owner refuses to refund a learning crew: that is one more call site to change. Not verified: how `close_year` and exit treat a producer with losses in a learning year; the rule needs it not to shed the plant in the first learning years (exit already "no longer waits on LOSS_YEARS_BEFORE_EXIT", producers_close.py:26-28), so the exit test must also read the level pair for a crew still learning.

**Why it avoids both errors**
- *Trap:* in the first year a level multiplier is the discounted mean over the path, not 5.0. Plain arithmetic for a 20-year horizon, 5 per cent rate: with 3 runs a year per workshop the level multiplier is 2.17; with 10 runs, 1.63; with 50 runs, 1.34; at 20 per cent rate and 10 runs, 2.17. So the labour bill is judged at roughly 1.3 to 2.2 times the booked, not 5. A recipe that is profitable when practised at ordinary margins gets a positive share and runs, practises, and the next year's multiplier is lower (the rule re-reads the crew each year).
- *Hopeless recipe:* if revenue is below the practised cost (floor hours, full yield), the level cost exceeds revenue in every year of the path, so `share_working` of the level pair is small for ever and the producer does not run at a loss indefinitely; if revenue sits between practised cost and the level cost, the result depends on the horizon and the rate, as it should (a higher rate or shorter horizon gives up sooner: investment is discounted). Learning-year losses are bounded by cash and credit, and the practice a loser buys is only the pace of the runs it can afford.
- *Continuity:* every quantity is a smooth function of the inputs: the multiplier is a power law with a floor (kinks only at the floor), the level value is a weighted mean, `share_working` is a CDF. The only discreteness is the whole-year steps already in `crew_runs_after_year` and the 0.01 rounding of multipliers in `recipe_for` (practice.py:60, 132).
- *Rules of the project:* no content ids; any actor with a `crew_runs` table (a firm, a state producer) uses it; no price table; the real-world mechanism is investment, not a hardcoded outcome; a shortage of skill does not silently stop everything: it slows the pace and prices the hours instead.

**Not changed:** the learning curve, the spoilage share, entry sizing, `entrant_crew_runs`. The first-ever producer still starts at 5x; the rule makes it run.

## Tests (one function called, inputs written out)

Functions are plain, per the project rule ("a test calls one function"). Assert on values, no wording.
1. `learning_path_scales(units_by_trade={"smith": 1.0}, runs_per_workshop_per_year=10.0, horizon_years=20, discount_rate=0.05)` returns a level multiplier equal to the hand-computed discounted mean (about 1.63, from the same curve; the test writes out the curve inputs: exponent from `LEARNING_RATE` 0.8, floor 0.2); asserts it lies strictly between 1.0 and `hours_multiplier(1.0)` = 5.0.
2. Same call at `units_by_trade={"smith": 148.4}` (floor) returns exactly 1.0 and share 1.0 (established crew unchanged, so no behaviour change for the opening).
3. Monotone and continuous: with the pace raised from 3 to 10 to 50 the level multiplier strictly falls; raising the discount rate from 0.05 to 0.20 raises it; a pace change of 1 per cent moves it by less than 1 per cent of itself.
4. With `horizon_years=1` the level multiplier equals `hours_multiplier(units)` (the old behaviour is the one-year limit).
5. The decision pair, in its own function (pull `share_working(level_revenue, level_variable, spread)` out, per the project rule): revenue per run 100, input cost 20, booked labour cost 40, level multiplier 1.6 (level variable 84) gives a share above 0.5; the same with a first-year multiplier 5.0 (variable 220) gives a share near 0; with revenue 70 and the practised cost 60 and level cost 84 the share is below 0.5 and falls as revenue falls: the hopeless-recipe case where practised cost 90 > revenue 70 gives a share below the same share at practised cost 60 (monotone), and below 0.01 when level cost is 150% of revenue. All numbers are inputs written out in the test.
6. Entry/plan agreement: `planned_recipe` and the plan's level pair give the same hours for the same crew, horizon and rate.
7. Integration (declared SMOKE, slow tier only): a one-recipe economy with no existing producer and a priced output above practised cost runs in its first year, `crew_runs` grows each year, the multiplier falls; the same economy with output priced below practised cost does not enter or runs to nothing within a stated number of years.

## Open points

- **Pace for the path.** Capacity per workshop is optimistic when capacity is large relative to demand. Alternative: expected sales. I chose capacity because `stock_runs` and the share decide the actual runs and using them would make the rule circular. Needs one measurement of a pre/post run on Mexica (I did not run anything).
- **Horizon constant has no sourced number.** I opened no figure for how long a producer expects to keep a trade; the apprenticeship term lengths in Wallis p. 23-24 are the only anchors seen and they are the learner's term, not the master's horizon. Label as a heuristic with the retire named above.
- **Yield spoilage in the level pair** uses `SCRAPPED_SHARE_OF_EXTRA_EFFORT`; stays as is.
- **Exit and restake** (entry.py:88-93, `producers_close.close_year`) must read the level pair for a crew still learning, otherwise a learning producer is shed or unfunded in year two. I did not read `close_year` and `year_close.py` beyond the grep lines; verify before implementing.
- **Who pays in the real world is the learner.** The code has no apprentice as a separate payer; in this simulator the producer's owner bears the learning loss through the stake. A closer fit to Wallis/Epstein would let hired learners take a rising wage scale (labour module); that belongs to Complaint 644, not this fix.
- **Option (b) peers elsewhere.** Real-world basis: Epstein p. 702 (permanent emigration of masters, temporary migration of journeymen carry technique); the Murano example and journeymen tramping are in the earlier repository report (not re-opened). The code already does the mean-of-peers start (`entrant_crew_runs`, practice.py:186-192, mirrored in `entry_practice.entrant_crew`), and `entrant_units` cites Eurace's regional-mean-skill restart. What it does not do: carry skill from a peer *elsewhere* (another area/civilisation) when there is no local peer: `crews_by_recipe` is the recipe's whole producer table, so "peers" already means all producers of the recipe anywhere in the record; I did not check whether the record spans civilisations. It also does not model that a migrant brings skill only if the trade's people move (that is `sim/labour/trade_formation.py`, migration then conversion, and `trade_first_hands.starting_proficiency`, which gives a converted cohort the covered share of tasks). Linking a first-ever producer's starting units to the proficiency of the cohort `trade_formation` delivers (instead of 1.0) is the realistic part of (b) and is independent of this rule: do it later under Complaint 644.
- Becker, Humphries and Minns were not opened; Spence below-cost pricing claim has no source opened (only the abstract).
