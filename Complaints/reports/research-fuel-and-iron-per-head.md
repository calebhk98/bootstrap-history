# Research: fuel for heat and iron per head, woodland yield, labour to cut firewood

Date of research: 2026-11-20. Web reading only, by a research agent; every figure below was read in an opened page or
PDF in that session unless it is listed under "Not found". The orchestrating session opened one of the citations
itself (King, chapter 8: the per-head figures for about 1500 and 1590 are in the text as quoted). Why it was asked:
Complaint 473 Update 45 found the settled England burning and buying far more firewood and steel a head than any
pre-industrial economy did, because warmth and implements have no upper level in `data/world/needs.json`.

## 1. Fuel for heat a head a year

**Warde, Paul, "Energy Consumption in England & Wales 1560-2000" (CNR, 2007), section 2.3, p. 39.**
URL opened: https://histecon.fas.harvard.edu/energyhistory/data/Warde_Energy Consumption England.pdf

- "even in the reign of Elizabeth I, it is doubtful that much more than 1 cubic metre of wood per person per annum
  could be supplied - certainly a historically low figure by northern European standards."
- "a supply of around 2.7 million cubic metres in 1550, or not far off one cubic metre per person per year from
  woodland, even at an assumed high rate of exploitation."
- This is a ceiling on supply, not a measured demand: "There are no wide-ranging surveys of household and industrial
  consumption of firewood... nigh on impossible to come to a definitive national figure."

**Allen and Weisdorf, "Was there an 'industrious revolution' before the industrial revolution?", University of
Copenhagen Discussion Paper 2010-14, Table 1 "Basket of Goods", citing Allen (2001).**
URL opened: https://www.economics.ku.dk/research/publications/wp/dp_2010/1014.pdf/

- Row "Fuel 5.0 millions BTU" under "Quantity/Person/year": a subsistence allowance. In joules (the agent's
  conversion at 1.055 kJ a BTU) about 5.3 GJ a head a year.

## 2. Iron a head a year (England)

**King, Peter, PhD thesis on the English iron industry, chapter 8 "Economic Trends and the Consumption of Iron".**
URL opened: https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk/catalogue/adsdata/arch-405-1/dissemination/pdf/thesis_text/8_Economic_Trends_and_the_Consumption_of_Iron.pdf

- Method: "'consumption per head' is estimated by dividing domestic consumption by population."
- About 1500: "English production may possibly have been 1000 tons per year in total and consumption 4 lb. or so per
  person per year."
- 1590: "about 9 lb. per head". 1620 to the 1660s: "averaging 8 lb. per head p.a." Later: "12 lb. in the early
  1720s to around 15½ lb. from the early 1760s", and more than 20 lb. about 1805.
- At 0.4536 kg a pound: about 1.8 kg a head about 1500, 4 kg in 1590, 9 kg about 1805. Bar iron, not steel alone; no
  figure for 1300.

## 3. Woodland yield and area

**Warde (as above), pp. 34-35.**

- "Today, the annual increment in broadleaf forest in England is 3.3 cubic metres per hectare. Collins estimates that
  early modern yields were around 3.6 cubic metres of stacked wood per hectare... or 2.25 metres cubed of solid
  cordwood."
- "According to estimates made in the Forest of Dean in the 1690s, coppiced woodland yielded about 4 cubic metres
  per hectare."
- England and Wales woodland fell "from around 900,000 hectares" (1550) "to 769,000 hectares by 1905".

**Royal Forestry Society, "7. A Brief History of British Woodlands".**
URL opened: https://rfs.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/7.-A-Brief-History-of-British-Woodlands.pdf

- "The Domesday Book records wood-pasture and woodland covering about 15% of England. However, the following two and
  a half centuries saw an expanding population and further clearance of woodland, halving woodland cover to about 7%."

## 4. Labour to cut firewood

**Low Tech Institute, "Firewood: Saw and Axe vs. Chainsaw and Splitter" (2021).** One author's extrapolation, sawing
and splitting only.
URL opened: https://lowtechinstitute.org/2021/04/26/firewood-saw-and-axe-vs-chainsaw-and-splitter-low-tech-r-d/

- Sawing "would have taken me about twenty-five hours by hand" a cord, and "a full cord of wood could be split by hand
  in four and a half hours". A cord is "4 ft x 8 ft x 4 ft of stacked, split wood". Felling and hauling are not in it,
  and the article gives no weight, so hours a tonne need a density that was not sourced.

## 5. Iron goods a household held (second pass, 2026-11-23)

Asked for: the weight of iron and other metal goods a pre-industrial household held, poor and well-off, and how long
such goods lasted, so that the implements need could be bounded by a stock a head as the vessels need is. The
orchestrating session opened the Roman figure's page itself (the table's wording is as quoted).

- **A stock a household or a head held: no source found.** The probate-inventory studies (Overton and others;
  Weatherill; Dyer; Briggs and Tompkins) could be reached only as catalogue pages; from search summaries alone, not
  opened, inventories list tools by count and value and not by weight.
- **A flow a head a year, Roman Britain.** Wikipedia, "Roman economy", section "Mining and metallurgy", table "Annual
  metal production in metric tons" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_economy, opened): iron "82,500 t", comment
  "Based on estimate of iron production at 1.5 kg per head in Roman Britain, extrapolated to population size of 55
  million for entire empire". An assumption extrapolated from one province; the works the page cites for it were not
  opened.
- **Single objects.** Kulturhistorisk museum, "The Langeid broadaxe"
  (https://khm.uio.no/english/collections/objects/the-langeid-broadaxe.html, opened): "an original weight of around
  800 grams (now 550 grams)", a two-handed war axe. O'Neill and others, EXARC Journal 2022/2
  (https://exarc.net/ark:/88735/10639, opened): replica ard shares show "significant signs of wear on the shares
  already over the course of a few years", one pictured "after five years of regular use". No weight given.
- **A service life of iron tools, pots or edged weapons in years: no source found.**
- **Copper-alloy goods: no source found.**

With the flows of this note (one to two kilograms of iron a head a year, sections 2 and 5) and any service life of
ten to twenty years, the iron in use a head comes to a few tens of kilograms; the life is not sourced, so this is an
order of magnitude and not a figure for the data.

## 6. What bar iron cost in days of work (third pass, 2026-11-26)

Asked for: the price of iron and steel in England about 1300 beside a wage of the same years, and the share of a
household's spending that went on tools and metal wares. Two pages were opened, by the research pass and again by a
separate fetch, and the quotes below are as both read them.

- **Bar iron, 1275 and 1285.** Dowsing Archaeology, "Historic Availability, Characteristics and Use of Wrought
  Iron", chapter 4 (http://www.dowsingarchaeology.org.uk/Ironwork/Chapter%204/4.%20History.htm, opened): Spanish
  iron "in 1275 cost 3s.1d/ cwt when brought to Canterbury"; "Iron from Pont-Audemer in Normandy was being bought in
  Norwich in 1285 for 15s.6d/ cwt." The page cites "(Salzman LF. 1952 p286)", which was not opened, and does not say
  what a hundredweight of iron weighed. The second price is five times the first and the page does not say why.
- **Wages, 1291 to 1350.** Medieval Price List, University of California, Davis
  (https://medieval.ucdavis.edu/120D/Money.html, opened), a chart "from Dyer ([3], p. 206). These are averages of
  daily wages in pence": 1291-1300, thatcher 2.5 and thatcher's mate 1; 1311-20, 3 and 1.25; 1321-30, 3 and 1;
  1331-40 and 1341-50, 3 and 1.25. The same list has "Laborer £2/year max c1300", source Dyer page 29. Its source
  [3] is Dyer, "Standards of Living in the Later Middle Ages" (1989), which was not opened.
- **Steel, at any date: no source found.**
- **The share of a household's spending on tools, metal wares or arms: no source found.** Dyer's and Allen's
  budgets could not be opened.
- **The weight of iron goods a holding held and the years a tool lasted: no source found** (as in section 5).

Worked from the figures above, with one assumption that no source here gives, a hundredweight of 112 pounds
(50.8 kilograms): the Canterbury iron of 1275 cost 37 pence a hundredweight, about three quarters of a penny a
kilogram, which is about three quarters of a day of a thatcher's mate at a penny a day in the 1290s and under a
third of a day of the thatcher. The Norwich iron of 1285 comes to about five times that. The prices and the wages
are a decade or two apart.

Against the model: a kilogram of steel in the settled England costs a few hours of the average wage (the wage of
`python3 sim/simulator.py spin-up-trace --civ england_1300 --rules clearing --stages`, steel's price from the
handoff's `probes/good_orders_by_kind.py`). Bar iron at most of a labourer's day a kilogram is dearer than that,
and steel was dearer than bar iron, so the model's steel is too cheap by a few times at the least. That is far
short of the gap in what is bought (section 2 against Update 50 of Complaint 473): the rest is the implements need
taking a fifth of spending above the floors with no upper level.

## What follows for the data (not yet done)

  incomes, so they are checks on its settled state and not levels to write into the needs.

  not yet read as a yearly growth (owner question 16 of `473-owner-questions.md` covers the ground forest recipes take).
