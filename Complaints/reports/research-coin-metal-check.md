## What the check does now (file:line)

- The check is built in `sim/engine/opening_checks_run.py:79-94` (`check_game`): it steps the opened game one year, then calls `opening_checks.price_over_cost("price_over_cost_" + group, ...)` for the groups `sited_ores`, `staple`, `coin_metal` (lines 89-90).
- The figures come from `price_ratios` (`opening_checks_run.py:62-76`):
  - For the coin metal the good is `opened["backing_good"]` from the `agent_opening_figures` view (line 70).
  - The numerator is `prices[good]`, the view `agent_prices` (the economy's shown price, i.e. the market price, NOT the mint's parity). The denominator is `costs[good]`, the view `material_prices` (the making cost through the recipes: ore, fuel, labour). Ratio = `prices[good] / costs[good]` (line 74).
  - A good enters only if `opening_checks.unmeasured_cause` (`sim/engine/opening_checks.py:42-60`) finds no cause: it must have a price, a making cost, not be in the stale list (market did not clear), have a price of kind `PRICE_SMOOTHED_TRADE` (a trade, not a quote or a thin-market blend), and have been made last year (`output`). Otherwise the group has no ratio and the check FAILS ("a check that measured nothing has not passed", `opening_checks.py:71-73`). This is why Han reads n/a (Complaint 970, line 38).
- The band: `PRICE_OVER_COST_BOUND = 10.0` (`opening_checks.py:15-18`). `price_over_cost` (`opening_checks.py:63-76`) passes when every ratio lies in [1/10, 10]; the reported value is the ratio furthest from one in log terms (line 74). The comment at lines 15-17 justifies 10 as "the widest grade spread among one metal's deposits, the most scarcity rent on a sited ore can be (Complaints/519)". That reason is about ores at deposits, not about a money metal.
- Same figures, same function, three groups: sited ores, the staple (`agent_staple`), the coin metal. Only the coin-metal group concerns this memo.

  - `sim/tests/test_opening_checks.py` (tests of the pure function; not opened in detail).

## Real-world evidence (sources opened)

1. **Mill, Principles of Political Economy, Book III ch. IX, "Of the Value of Money, as Dependent on Cost of Production"** [opened: https://www.econlib.org/library/Mill/mlP.html?chapter_num=41].
   - "The ultimate regulator of its value is Cost of Production." (section 1)
   - Natural value of the metals is "in the long run proportional to their cost of production ... at the worst mine which it is necessary to work in order to obtain the required supply." (section 2) -- i.e. the MARGINAL mine, not the average.
   - "there is at all times a very large quantity of these metals in existence" and "a comparatively small annual production is sufficient to keep up the supply." (section 2) -- the stock dominates the flow.
   - Adjustment is through the general price level and slow: "If gold is above its natural or cost value ... the prices of all things, labour included, will be low"; "the adjustment takes a long time to effect, in the case of a commodity so generally desired"; "Alterations ... in the cost of production of the precious metals, do not act upon the value of money except just in proportion as they increase or diminish its quantity." (sections 2-3)
   - Above cost, miners "will obtain extra profits, and will be stimulated to increase his production"; below cost they "would slacken their works"; "some of the inferior mines would perhaps stop working altogether." (the fetch tool reported that Mill's text does not use the word rent in this chapter; I did not independently confirm.)
   - Reading: supports (a) at the margin only and says nothing numeric about the gap.
2. **Fernandez-Villaverde and Sanches, "Price-Level Determination Under the Gold Standard", Philadelphia Fed WP 24-06, 2024** [opened, full text via pdftotext, held in my scratchpad].
   - A modern micro-founded commodity-money model; "it ensures long-term price stability as the quantity of money and prices only temporarily deviate from their steady-state levels" (abstract). It abstracts from domestic mining: "Although we do not consider the implications of domestic mining of precious metals ..." and points to Choi and Rocheteau (2021, 2022) for that extension (p. 7). It cites Sargent and Wallace 1983 and Velde and Weber 2000 as earlier commodity-money models (p. 6). Reading: the modern literature takes the money stock and price level as adjusting, with the metal's price pinned by the standard; it offers no cost-gap bound.
3. **Choi and Rocheteau, "Money Mining and Price Dynamics", AEJ Macro 13(4), 2021, 246-94** [snippet only: abstract seen in search]. Money mined with a time-consuming technology; the model admits price paths where the price of money rises and collapses gradually. Reading: persistent departures from a simple cost anchor are possible in theory.
4. **Bordo, "Gold Standard", Concise Encyclopedia of Economics (Econlib)** [opened]. "new production of gold would add only a small fraction to the accumulated stock"; after the 1848-1850 discoveries "the initial effect of the gold discovery was to increase real output ... eventually the full effect was on the price level alone." Reading: the price level absorbs a supply shock over years.
5. **Barro 1979 (Economic Journal 89(353)) and Rockoff 1984 ("Some evidence on the real price of gold, its costs of production, and commodity prices", in Bordo and Schwartz, A Retrospective on the Classical Gold Standard, pp. 613-650)** [NOT OPENED; the NBER PDF is a scan with no text layer]. A search summary says that Rockoff tests the cost-of-production ("metallic") theory using the real price of gold = mint price over the commodity price level. I cannot state their findings. No source found for the speed of output response I would want to quote.
6. **Erb and Harvey, "The Golden Constant", Journal of Investing, Spring 2017** [opened, pdftotext]. After 1975 the monthly real price of gold (nominal over CPI) averaged about 3.46, with a high near 8.73 and a low near 1.47. They state the hypothesis "the price of gold is driven by the cost of production of gold mining companies" only to question it. Reading: in the fiat era the real price of gold is not tied to production cost over four decades; but that is a floating price, not a price fixed at a mint, so it is weak evidence for the model's Norse case.
7. **Xenophon, Poroi (Ways and Means) 4** [opened: https://stuff.mit.edu/afs/athena/course/21/21h.401/www/local/xenophon_poroi.html]. Nicias "once owned a thousand men in the mines" and let them to Sosias "on condition that Sosias paid him an obol a day per man net" (4.14); and "if each man brings in a clear obol a day, the annual revenue ... is sixty talents" (4.23, for six thousand men). Also "the ore will never give out and ... silver will never lose its value" (4.11) and "Neither is silver like furniture" (4.7). Reading: in classical Athens the owner of the labour was paid a fixed net sum per worker per day by the operator; a rent-like transfer from the mine's operator to its factor owner. It does not give the mine's total margin.
8. **Aristotle, Athenaion Politeia 47 (Perseus translation)** [opened]: the officials "farm out all public contracts and sell the mines and the taxes", with "the mines sold and the workings that have been sold for three years and the concessions sold for ... years" (number half erased). Reading: the Athenian state sold mine concessions for a price, so the deposit's owner (the state) captured a rent at sale. No price figure.
9. **Quinto real, Wikipedia** [opened, weak source]: "reserves to the monarch 20% of all precious metals"; for Spanish mining reduced "to a diezmo (10%)" in 1723 and "to 3%" in 1777. **Potosi, Wikipedia** [opened, weak]: "the quinto, a 20% severance tax on gross value"; "By 1565, the miners had exhausted the direct-smelting ore, and silver production plummeted"; ore up to 40% silver at peak; patio (mercury amalgamation) process let "lower-grade ores" be worked. Reading: a 20 percent take on gross value was sustainable in the Spanish empire's richest mines for a long time, so producers could pay at least that much over their working cost; the later cut to 10 and 3 percent is consistent with falling margins as ore worsened, but the page does not state that cause (the fetch said so).
10. **Kutna Hora and Laurion, Wikipedia** [opened, weak]: Laurion "some businessmen decided to rent out some of these recently abandoned mines" about 370 BCE, "more successful than expected" (a boom about 340 BCE): marginal mines can be re-let and become profitable when conditions change. Kutna Hora: royal mining code about 1300; mining reduced in the 1530s partly from "the threat of flooding in the deepest mines" (cost rising). The page gives NO royalty rate or royal revenue for Kutna Hora. A search snippet says parts of the mines were "leased for a share of the extracted ore" (not opened).
11. **Bettenay 2022 (Metalla 26.2)**: could not be re-fetched this session (Anubis block); cited only through `precious-metal-mining-sources.md`.

Not found, so absent here: a figure for Rammelsberg; a sourced royalty or tenth for Kutna Hora; a sourced total profit margin of any ancient or medieval silver mine over its working cost; a measured time to return to cost after a gold or silver supply shock. Marked "no source found" for each.

**What the evidence supports, in plain terms**
- Classical theory (Mill, opened) puts the money metal's long-run value at the cost of the worst mine that must be worked, and puts the adjustment in the price level over a long time because the stock is huge against a year's output. A fixed mint price therefore does NOT pin the metal's cost to its price year by year. In the first years the gap is whatever the history left; it only closes (or the price level moves) over decades.
- Mines above the margin earn more than cost. Mill implies it ("worst mine"); Athenian practice (mine concessions sold, slaves hired out at a net obol a day) and the Spanish quinto (20 percent on gross value, kept for centuries) show owners and crowns capturing a share. This is a rent to the deposit's owner, not a loss of anyone.
- I found no opened source that bounds the size of that margin. So a ceiling like "10 times cost" has no source for a money metal.

## Recommended check

The recommendation is for a money metal bought at a fixed price (civilisation whose spec has `mint_price.paid_in_metal(spec)` true: struck coin and weighed metal). In plain words for the owner: the mint pays one unit of money for one unit of silver. That price must be enough to make the silver (that is the real-life "cost of the worst mine that has to be worked"). It does NOT have to be close to the cost, because the better mines make more than cost. So the question to ask is "is parity at least what making the silver costs?", not "is the market price near cost?".

**Check name and the quantities (spelled out)**
- `paid_price_per_unit_of_metal` = what the regime pays per unit of the backing good, `mint_price.paid_price(spec)` (parity less the regime's charge; for weighed metal, parity).
- `making_cost_per_unit_of_metal` = `material_prices[backing_good]` (the same figure the present check uses as `costs[good]`, `opening_checks_run.py:67,74`).
- `mint_cover_over_cost` = `paid_price_per_unit_of_metal` divided by `making_cost_per_unit_of_metal`.
- PASS when `mint_cover_over_cost >= 1.0` (parity covers the making cost). Floor 1.0 comes from Mill (opened) -- the value of the metal is the cost of the worst mine worked; it is not a tuned number. FAIL (not "n/a") if cost or paid price is missing, as `price_over_cost` does now.
- NO upper bound. Detail fields (reported, never fail): `market_price_over_paid_price` = `agent_prices[backing_good] / paid_price_per_unit_of_metal` (a mint's band says this should sit between the mint's buying price and its selling price, `economy-research-extraction-and-money-metals.md` section 3 [memory]), and `paid_price_over_cost_by_year` for the spin-up years if the trace carries it (it does not today as far as I saw; unverified).
- Where the bound comes from: floor = Mill's marginal-cost theory (Book III ch. IX section 2, opened). Upper side: nothing sourced, so nothing asserted. If the owner still wants a ceiling it must be a labelled heuristic in code (`retire`: the complaint that adds a deposit cost curve, i.e. mechanism 1 below), and I would keep the present `PRICE_OVER_COST_BOUND` of 10 only in the detail, not as a pass condition.
- Time span: the floor is read once on the opened game after the first year, as the present check is. No trend assertion over the hidden spin-up years (see candidate (c) below).

**For non-money uses and other civilisations**
- A civilisation whose money is not a metal (commodity money like Mexica's cacao: `paid_in_metal` false, `mint_price.py:81-84`; or no coin metal): the new check does not apply (report "not applicable", not a pass); its coin-metal group, if any, keeps the existing `price_over_cost("..._coin_metal")` band 0.1-10, because for a good bought at ordinary demand the market price and cost should stay within an order of magnitude and nothing fixes the price.
- Struck coin with a charge (Rome, England, Han as the specs say; I did not read `currency.py` for which regimes each uses): the new check applies with the lower mint price (parity less charge); the market price may then sit between the buying and selling price.
- The ores and staple groups keep the present band untouched (they are not bought at a fixed price).
- A civilisation can have both groups: the old `price_over_cost_coin_metal` does not make sense for a metal at a fixed price, so for those civilisations it is replaced, not added to.

**Candidates weighed**
- (a) Parity covers the marginal producer's cost; intramarginal producers earn rent. SUPPORTED: Mill (marginal mine sets value; extra profits above it), Athenian concession sales and the quinto (rent captured by the owner). Implementable today for the floor half. The intramarginal-rent half cannot be tested because the model has no cost curve (next section).
- (b) Growth of the money stock from mining stays within a stated range of the stock. PARTLY SUPPORTED in direction: Mill ("a comparatively small annual production is sufficient"), Bordo ("only a small fraction to the accumulated stock"), World Gold Council modern figure of about 2 percent a year (from `gold-silver-value-research.md`, read there; modern). NO opened source for an ancient or medieval range, and Roman or Norse numbers would be historical outcomes (CLAUDE.md 4.1/4.2). This is what `money_against_wear` already tests from the other side (the stock does not wear away; `opening_checks.py:79-94`); an upper limit on growth would be a new bound without a source. I recommend not adding it until the model can show the stock-to-flow ratio and a source is read.
- (c) Gap between parity and the average making cost does not widen over the spin-up. NOT SUPPORTED as a pass/fail: Mill says convergence is slow ("takes a long time"), the model has no depletion that raises cost (see below), and spin-up years are not decades; a gap that stays flat is what the model would produce, and the check would then fail for a reason the model cannot yet fix. It would also tie the check to the unsettled spin-up (473).

## What the mechanism needs

For the real mechanism (price level moves, or cost rises toward parity over decades) to work in the model, these must exist. I only say what I saw in the code; I do not design.

1. **A cost that rises as the best seams are worked out (deposits as a cost curve, not one cost).**
   - The depletion model exists as a standalone: `sim/world/deposits.py` documents `current_ore_grade_kg_per_tonne(deposit, fraction_extracted)` at `:876` (grade falls as the deposit is worked) and `simulate_depletion` at `:1425`. A grep over `sim/` outside `world/deposits.py` and `tests/` found no caller of either, so I did not find it wired into the economy's yearly loop (unverified beyond that grep).
   - The live path: `sim/markets/port_sites.py:43-57` `site_limits` gives each ore recipe a per-tile capacity from `room_tonnes_per_year` (a capacity cap, not a rising cost); `:60-85` `deplete` draws raised ore from deposits "with most left first" (`remaining_tonnes`), reducing remaining tonnes but with no change in grade that I saw, so the making cost of the next tonne does not rise. `sim/resources/deposits.py:99-135` lists `grade_kg_per_tonne` per deposit row; whether the recipe cost reads that grade after depletion I did not verify.
   - `material_prices` returns one making cost per good, so "the marginal producer's cost" and "the average" are not distinguishable by this check; I did not verify how it is built from the recipes.
2. **Entry of miners while parity exceeds marginal cost, and exit when it does not.** Entry exists in general: `sim/economy/entry.py` (entrants sized to unfilled bids, owner's stake; `entry_margin`, `entry_sizing` named in `473-owner-questions.md:113`) and the idle/close behaviour from `economy-research-extraction-and-money-metals.md` is a plan (state-machine) -- I did not verify it is built. Whether entry into a site-limited ore is blocked by `room_tonnes_per_year` (capacity cap) was not checked; in a site-limited market a high price attracts no new producers, only a higher ore price, which is the Complaint 519 pattern.
3. **A rent paid to the deposit's owner.** The economy has `land_rent` per tile feeding the producer's variable cost (`sim/economy/close_kernel.py:143,181,287`; `sim/economy/api.py:150-163`; `land_market.py`, `titles_value.py`). I did not verify that ore deposits are rented or owned (a title on a deposit, a state share, a quinto-like charge). Without it, the margin between price and cost has nowhere to go but the miner's profit and the ore price, which is why the ore site "takes the margin" (519-history line 110).
4. **A price level that answers to the metal stock.** `sim/coin/prices.py:38-67` `revalue_coin_metals` (damped) and `sim/world/coin_metal.py` `revalued_ratio` revalue the coin metal against goods; `test_coin_metal_revaluation.py:28` ("a glut of the coin metal raises the price level") and `test_coin_revaluation_runaway.py` exist. That is the Mill channel. Whether it acts on the opening's wage and wheat prices is the open "price level" lead in 519 (unverified).
5. **A labour cost of silver of the right size.** 519's note that model silver takes "a few hundred" hours per kg against thousands attested (Complaint 349) means `making_cost_per_unit_of_metal` may be understated; a floor test `mint_cover_over_cost >= 1` passes more easily with an understated cost, so the check could pass for a wrong reason. From `precious-metal-mining-sources.md` (Bettenay Table 3): about 1.4 to 2.33 kg silver per worker-year implies about 1,000 to 1,700 hours per kg for whole-workforce productivity (the report's conversion, not mine).
