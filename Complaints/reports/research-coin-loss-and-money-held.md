# Research: coin loss, metal goods loss, and money held against income

Date of research: 2026-11-17. Web reading only. Every figure below was read in an opened page or PDF in this session unless it is listed under "Not found or not opened". Quotes are exact and short. Where a page was read through a fetch tool that summarises, that is stated and the quote is marked "(fetch-tool extract)". Arithmetic that is mine is marked "(my arithmetic)".

## 1. How fast coin lost metal or disappeared

### 1a. Weight loss by wear alone

**Velde, Francois R., "On the Evolution of Specie: Circulation and Weight Loss in 18th and 19th Century Coinage", Banque de France Working Paper 422, February 2013.**
URL opened: https://publications.banque-france.fr/sites/default/files/medias/documents/working-paper_422_2013.pdf (PDF downloaded; digits are missing from the text layer, so the tables were read from page images, page 5, 7, 20 and 25 of the paper).

- Table 5 (page 25), "annual loss (ppm)", ppm meaning parts per million of coin weight per year. Selected rows, average column:
  - United Kingdom gold, 1 pound face (the sovereign): 251.1 (1826 survey), 375.9 (1833), 366.7 (1868), 350.8 (1882), 313.7 (1888), 291.6 (1895). That is 0.025 to 0.038 percent a year (my arithmetic: 251 ppm = 0.0251 percent).
  - United Kingdom gold, half sovereign: 312.4 (1826) rising to 1130.4 (1868), 710.4, 851.3, 844.4. That is 0.03 to 0.11 percent a year (my arithmetic).
  - United Kingdom silver, largest listed denomination (6.3 French francs value): 169.5 (1798). Mid silver (3.2 francs): 1045.3 (1826), 1034.5 (1833), 1439.5 (1906-09). Small silver (1.3 francs): 2420.3 (1826), 2309.0 (1833), 2585.5 (1906-09). Smallest silver (0.6 and 0.3 francs): 2737 to 4002. That is 0.017 percent for the largest piece to 0.40 percent a year for the smallest silver pieces (my arithmetic).
- Page 20, France: "concluded that weight loss was linear in time and averaged 4mg per year (160 ppm)" for 5 franc silver pieces (Dumas commission, 1839 report); and "Costes (1885, 57) reports experiments made in the Paris mint, showing that pre-Revolution ecus had lost 1.95% in 1816, 2.19% in 1822, and 2.44% in 1828, suggesting an annual loss of 410ppm per year".
- Page 7: "bronze 10% less" susceptible to abrasion than silver, and "gold being 25-33% more susceptible to abrasion than silver" (for the 19th century alloys). No absolute bronze rate was found in the pages read.
- Page 5: "wear is linear in the logarithm of coin value" (abstract), meaning small coins lose a larger share of their weight than large coins. All the data are nineteenth century milled coin, which wears slower than hammered coin.

**Jevons through the Mint report. "Report addressed to the Chancellor of the Exchequer by the Master of the Mint and Colonel Smith ...", 28 June 1869, reproduced by the World Gold Council.**
URL opened: https://www.gold.org/sites/default/files/documents/1869jun28.pdf

- "Mr. Jevon's experiments and observations furnish the best data we possess for estimating the annual loss by wear on 100 sovereigns. It is 8.371 pence."
- Same paragraph set: whole currency "taken at 80 millions" gives an annual loss of "27,903l.6s.8d."; with half sovereigns "35,000l."
- Conversion (my arithmetic): 100 sovereigns face value is 24,000 pence, so 8.371 pence is 0.035 percent a year. This agrees in order of magnitude with Velde's 0.025 to 0.038 percent for the sovereign.
- Paragraph 1 and 7: a sovereign is withdrawn when light, and the Mint plans renewal of "1-18th part" of whole sovereigns each year and "1-10th part" of half sovereigns.

**Jevons, W. S., "Money and the Mechanism of Exchange" (1875), as quoted in a search result from econlib.org.** Only a search snippet was seen, the page was not opened: "often one-fourth or one-third of its original weight" lost by worn coin in circulation. Treated as not opened (see below).

**Ball, Katherine, DPhil thesis, University of Oxford, "The Role of Demographic and Monetary Factors in the Late Medieval Economies of England, Scotland and the Southern Low Countries (1351-1530)", 2018.**
URL opened: https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:2083b7e4-26d4-4116-b1be-d339e980db9c/files/mbcf804657aa89b31c5c55218e838eab6 (PDF, read as text).

- "economic historians have estimated that wear, tear and wastage of silver coins amounted to between 1% and 4% per decade." Footnote cites Mayhew 1974 "Numismatic Evidence" pp. 2-3, Mayhew 2012, Allen "Mints and Money" pp. 333-5, van Hengel 1982, and Lloyd. The sources behind it were not opened.
- Conversion (my arithmetic): 1 percent per decade is 0.10 percent a year; 4 percent per decade is 0.41 percent a year. This is the same range as Velde's small silver pieces, and is about one tenth to one quarter of the 1 to 4 percent a year total loss rates in 1b. The phrase "per decade" is the thesis's own wording, and conflicts with the per year rates of the other sources only if both are read as the same quantity; they are not, since 1b is total disappearance.

### 1b. Total loss of the coin stock a year (loss, hoarding not recovered, melting, export)

**Lockyear, Kris, Sven Aagaard and Michael Marcher, "The Lohe hoard revisited" (about 2016, a UCL Discovery paper).**
URL opened: https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10171145/3/Lockyear_thordeman%20final.pdf (PDF, read as text).

- On Thordeman 1948 (Swedish mark coins, Stockholm hoard): "due to a variety of factors including accidental loss, hoarding and export, a systematic proportion of coins in circulation are lost each year, and for this period of history that loss rate was about 2%."
- Section 4.1, a review of earlier estimates: "Volk reviewed the topic in detail and estimated the loss rate for the Roman Republic at between 2.75% and 3%. Patterson's study looked at loss rates for a variety of periods and places and obtained estimates from between 1% and 4%, whereas Preston's study for the Roman Republic produced estimates between 2.5% and 4%. Iossif's paper uses a value of 1.5%, but also assess the impact of different rates. Lockyear's study, based on a simulation approach similar to that adopted here, suggested that an average rate of 2% was appropriate for Roman silver denarii."
- Same section: "an important point made by both Volk and Lockyear is that it is likely that the decay rate will vary with the denomination." Their own fits for individual denominations in the Lohe hoard ranged from 0.96 percent to 4.45 percent.
- This is the best single statement of the range, but it is a secondary report of Patterson (1972). Patterson himself was not opened (see below). Definition used by these authors: "removed from the coinage pool due to hoarding, recycling, or simple loss", a compound rate.

**Ball thesis (as above), pages near the 1452-1461 and 1386-1399 discussions.**
- Takes "a compounding loss/wastage of 2%" and also 4% a year as the two cases for the English silver stock, and writes that "the money stock actually declined by just 1.46% per annum" with 2 percent wastage once a balance of payments inflow is added. These are modelling assumptions, not measurements.

**Duncan-Jones, Richard, "Money and Government in the Roman Empire" (1994), chapter 13 "Weight-loss and circulation-speed" and the chapter on "Wastage and reminting of coin".**
URLs opened: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/money-and-government-in-the-roman-empire/weightloss-and-circulationspeed/B9A70254E3C367135FC2FB3297B38286 and https://cambridge.org/core/books/money-and-government-in-the-roman-empire/wastage-and-reminting-of-coin/72B6AD8DD00F5C8117F1AD0D48A1B985 (fetch-tool extracts; only the free summary).
- "In big samples, the results suggest that the rate of wear was approximately linear, as in modern evidence (n.33)." (fetch-tool extract). No percentage was visible. Lockyear et al. note that Duncan-Jones's estimates "fail to account for the growth of the coinage pool" (their footnote 32).

**Velde, working paper 422, page 20.** An assumption, not a measurement: "assuming a constant disappearance rate of 1% per year, the average coin was about 31 years old, yielding an average annual loss of 350ppm and 1200ppm" (the weight loss, not the disappearance, is the ppm figure).

### 1c. Metal goods (plate, ornament, weighed metal)

No source found giving a yearly loss rate for plate, jewellery or weighed silver. Nothing opened measures the metal goods loss. The only related figure is the wear of coin (1a), which does not apply to goods that are not handled as currency. The sovereign renewal plan above (1/18 a year of the sovereigns) is a legal replacement schedule for coin, not a loss of goods.

## 2. How much coin people held against yearly income

**England 1300, Mayhew's series, as shown on a slide by John Munro.** John H. Munro, lecture slides "Money and Prices ...", slide 21, "Mayhew on English Money Supplies, Prices, National Income, Velocity in millions (£ sterling & population)".
URL opened: https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/munro5/L04BMedPricesPopulationMoney.pdf (slide read as an image). The slide credits Mayhew; the underlying Mayhew 1995 article was not opened.
- Table for 1300: Money Supply 0.900 (millions of pounds), Income Velocity 5.178, National Income Y 4.660 (millions of pounds), Population 6.000 (millions).
- Check (my arithmetic): 4.660 / 0.900 = 5.18, matching the velocity shown. So the coin stock was 0.19 of a year's income, about 2.3 months of income, or about 3 shillings of coin per head against 15.5 shillings of income per head (0.150 pound per head of money, 0.777 per head of income).
- Other columns on the same slide: 1470 money 0.900, velocity 3.889, income 3.500; 1526 money 1.400, velocity 3.571, income 5.000; 1546 velocity 5.517; 1561 velocity 9.310; 1600 velocity 6.286; 1643 velocity 3.500; 1670 velocity 3.407. So the ratio of coin to income in England moved between about 0.1 and 0.3 across four centuries.
- Mayhew's own definition, as quoted by others: velocity is income over money stock ("Velocity (Y/M)"), seen in a search snippet for Mayhew 2022, not opened.

**England 1351 and 1422, per head coin.** Stevens, M., "London creditors and the fifteenth-century depression", Economic History Review 69(4), 2016, pre-publication draft. URL opened: https://cronfa.swansea.ac.uk/Record/cronfa27688/Download/0027688-02022018152745.pdf. Quote: "in 1351 there were about 56 pence in circulation per head of the population, but by 1422 this had fallen to just 13 pence per head." Gives no income comparison.

**England 1665, Petty.** William Petty, "Verbum Sapienti", written about 1665, published 1691, Wikisource edition of the 1899 Hull collection.
URL opened: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Verbum_Sapienti_(1899)/Chapters (fetch-tool extract).
- Chapter V: "the Expence being 40 Millions" and "if we have 5½ Millions we have enough" (fetch-tool extract). Chapter I: coined gold and silver "scarce worth six millions".
- Ratio (my arithmetic): 6 / 40 = 0.15 of a year of national expense, 5.5 / 40 = 0.14. This is a contemporary estimate, not a measurement, and the 40 million is annual spending, not a modern income total.

**Britain and France, 1870.** Velde (2013), page 5: "In 1870 the coin stock represented 8% of GDP in Britain, and 38% in France; by 1900 these figures were" (the rest not legible in the image). Industrial, gold-standard economies with banks and notes.

**Rome, mid second century AD.** Danon, Bart, "Roman coinage under the Antonines revisited", in "The Uncertain Past" (Cambridge, 2022).
URL opened: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/uncertain-past/roman-coinage-under-the-antonines-revisited/76626FBFC05664B712DC6ABE6D4790C8 (fetch-tool extract of the summary).
- "At more than 20 bn sesterces, his estimate implied an anomalously high monetization ratio" (the estimate is Duncan-Jones's). "Allowance for various uncertainties and other, minor components of the coinage suggests a money supply of around 16 bn sesterces." "However, this is not quite low enough to explain away the monetization ratio, implying a higher GDP and more trade-oriented economy than currently thought."
- GDP: Scheidel, Walter and Steven Friesen, "The size of the economy and the distribution of income in the Roman Empire", Journal of Roman Studies 99 (2009). URL opened (PDF): https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/ADBB8C20D8DFDB7F5A8B209718AF7942/S0075435800000071a.pdf/size_of_the_economy_and_the_distribution_of_income_in_the_roman_empire.pdf. Quote: "total income approaching the equivalent of 50 million tons of wheat or close to 20 billion sesterces per year". Also "a GDP of 43.4m tons translates to between HS13bn and HS20bn".
- Ratio (my arithmetic, combining two authors with different methods and the same period): 16 / 20 = 0.8, more than 20 / 20 = 1.0 or more. Danon himself calls the high ratio a problem, so this should be read as an unresolved range, not a measurement. The money figures cover centrally minted coin; the GDP is for the whole empire, including output that was never monetised.

**Han China.** No coin stock against output was found.
- Wikipedia, "Han dynasty coinage", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty_coinage (opened): "From 118 BC to 5 AD, the government minted over 28,000,000,000 cash coins, with an annual average of 220,000,000 coins minted". It cites Nishijima 1986 (Cambridge History of China vol. 1, p. 586), which was not opened. This is cumulative mintage, not stock in circulation, and no output or population denominator was found, so no ratio is computed.

## 3. What households kept savings in, other than coin

**England 1665, Petty (Verbum Sapienti, Chapter I, fetch-tool extract, same URL as above).** Components of the "whole 250 millions":
- Land: "worth 144 Millions to be sold" (24 millions of rent-capitalised yield, 8 millions rent a year).
- Housing: "the Housing of England may be estimated worth 30 Millions."
- Shipping: "is worth three Millions".
- Cattle: "is worth ¼ of the said Land, viz. 36 millions".
- Coined gold and silver: "scarce worth six millions".
- Wares, plate, furnishings: "may be estimated at 31 millions".
- Shares (my arithmetic, against 250): land 58 percent, housing 12, livestock 14, wares plate and furnishings 12, coin 2.4, shipping 1.2. The share of plate alone was not separated.
- Caveat: a seventeenth century estimate by one author, 365 years after the model's England date. The Economic History Review summary (https://ehs.org.uk/article/measuring-the-national-wealth-in-seventeenth%e2%80%90century-england/) says Petty's estimate is the first national accounts and King's later one was "heavily influenced by it" (fetch-tool paraphrase), and says nothing on the breakdown.

**England, lay subsidies.** Nightingale, Pamela, "The lay subsidies and the distribution of wealth in medieval England, 1275-1334", Economic History Review 57 (2004). URL opened (abstract page only): https://ehs.org.uk/article/the-lay-subsidies-and-the-distribution-of-wealth-in-medieval-england-1275-1334/. Abstract statement (fetch-tool paraphrase with the phrase quoted): the discrepancies are explained by the "progressive exclusion from the tax valuations of wool, coin, and credit". So the subsidy rolls omit coin and credit and cannot give the share of wealth held as coin.

**England, credit and debt about 1300.** Campbell, Bruce M. S., "Factor markets in England before the Black Death" (draft, not to be cited without permission; read only for orientation). URL opened: https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/Research/GEHN/GEHNConferences/conf6/Conf6-BCampbell.pdf. Quote: "the total taxable wealth of the nation, both lay and ecclesiastical, was approximately £1.9 million" with registered unpaid debts of "£50,000" at the end of the thirteenth century if a fifth were unpaid. The context is lay taxable movables only, so it is not a measure of the composition of wealth. Given only as a scale check: Mayhew's coin stock for 1300 (0.9 million) is about half of that taxable-movables total (my arithmetic: 0.9 / 1.9 = 0.47), though the two are not measured on the same basis.

**Other statements.** Overton and others on probate inventories: a search snippet only (not opened); the snippet stated that cash and credit are underrecorded in inventories. Not usable as a source.

## What the sources support

Coin loss a year (model: 0.01 for abrasion, clipping, hoarding and burial):
- Wear alone: 0.02 to 0.04 percent a year for gold sovereigns and 0.2 to 0.4 percent for small milled silver (Velde 2013, Table 5; Mint report 1869: 0.035 percent, my arithmetic). Ball's "1 to 4 percent per decade" (0.1 to 0.4 percent a year, my arithmetic) fits that. Hammered, clipped medieval coin was not measured in any opened source. Firmness: good for nineteenth century milled coin, weak for the model's civilisations.
- Total disappearance of the coin stock: 1 to 4 percent a year, centre about 2 percent (Lockyear, Aagaard and Marcher quoting Patterson 1972, Volk, Preston, Iossif, Thordeman). Firmness: moderate, a range that several independent authors reach by fitting hoards, with higher rates for small denominations; the primary Patterson text was not opened. The model's 0.01 sits at the low end of this range and is about ten to forty times the wear-only rate.

Metal goods loss a year (model: 0.005):
- No source found. Nothing in the opened sources measures the loss of plate, ornament or weighed metal. The coin wear figures above are the nearest numbers and are not the same thing. Firmness: none.

Coin held against a year's income (model: a stock expressed against income):
- England about 1300: 0.19 of a year's income (money 0.9 million, income 4.66 million, velocity 5.18), moving between 0.1 and 0.3 from 1300 to 1670 (Mayhew series as shown on a Munro slide). England 1665: about 0.15 of a year's national expense (Petty). Britain 1870: 0.08 of GDP, France 0.38 (Velde). Firmness: moderate for medieval England, only one chain of estimates and read secondhand.
- Rome about 160 AD: roughly 0.8 to 1.0 or more (16 to over 20 billion sesterces of money against about 20 billion sesterces of GDP, my arithmetic), flagged by Danon himself as implausibly high. Firmness: weak.
- Han China, Norse, Mexica: no source found.
- Wealth held as coin: 2.4 percent of national wealth in England 1665 (Petty, my arithmetic); land 58 percent, livestock 14 percent, housing 12 percent, wares plate and furnishings 12 percent. English lay subsidy rolls exclude coin and credit, so they cannot give the share. Firmness: one contemporary estimate, outside the model's dates.
