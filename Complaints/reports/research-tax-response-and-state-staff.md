## 1. Tax response (Complaint 657)

### 1.1 Flight and anachoresis, Roman Egypt

All from Naphtali Lewis, "A Reversal of a Tax Policy in Roman Egypt", *Greek, Roman and Byzantine Studies* 34 (1993) 101-108
(open PDF, Duke GRBS). OPENED.

- p. 101: under the Ptolemies flight was "in the nature of a strike", workers waiting to negotiate a return. Under Rome
  "the flight was often a final solution", which Lewis puts down "principally" to "the pressures of taxes and liturgies,
  which were applied more ineluctably". Leaving one's registered abode (idia) made a person an outlaw (p. 101-102).
- p. 102: fugitives went "up" into the hills and lived by banditry, or made for cities to disappear into the crowd; "we have,
  of course, no statistics". Footnote 2 argues the papyri show flight as "essentially resulting from economic ruin or
  distress", against the view that it was a pull towards Alexandria.
- p. 102: Philadelphia (Arsinoite nome), last years of Claudius and first of Nero: loss through anachoresis of "more than ten
  per cent of its adult male population". Poll-tax farmers petitioned the prefect for a reduced contract because the
  villages "have been reduced to small numbers through some men having fled leaving no property and others having died".
  Lewis gives no tax rate and says the documents do not state the cause (footnote 3).
- p. 103: a partial tax forgiveness "induced forty-seven of the 152 missing men to return home" (mine: 47/152, about three in
  ten). Hadrianic relief for tenants of public and imperial land cut the tax in kind on parcels by "some fifty to eighty per
  cent" after the war of 115-117; a two to five year moratorium after two low floods (136).
- p. 103: "the whole community constituting the local tax unit (e.g. the village) was made collectively responsible"; under
  Trajan a new tax, merismos anakechorekoton, "annually prorated unpaid taxes of fugitives among the taxpayers remaining".
  Earliest attested 103, latest 161.
- p. 104: after a census proclamation (which carried an amnesty) the payment of a remaining taxpayer "was less than a tenth
  of what it had been the year before": fugitives returned within a year.
- p. 105: P. Thmouis 1, a village "fallen from 150 men to only forty-five men, of whom thirty-four had fled because
  impoverished and only eleven remained"; two villages "formerly populous, now fallen to only fourteen men, of whom ten have
  fled and only four remain".
- p. 106: Psenathre, 319 men registered in the census of 131/132, ten poll-tax payers left by about 162/163, "of whom eight
  have fled because, he says, being few they can not pay the whole". The prefect Syriacus (decision of January 163, p. 107)
  ended collective liability: the remainder "were required to pay only their own taxes"; where nobody remained, the tax was
  cancelled.
- p. 108: in 168 the prefect suspended the merismos "in order that those in flight may return to their homes and those still
  there may be able to remain".

What this gives: a feedback structure with a source (collective liability makes each remaining payer's take rise as the base
shrinks, so flight accelerates, until the state drops the rule or cancels the debt), and the fact that amnesty brings a
fraction back within about a year. What it does not give: a rate. No source states the share of the harvest or the poll-tax
amount at which flight began. The cases cited are villages that petitioned, so they are the worst cases (selection).

Other Egyptian papyri found:

- Adam Lukaszewicz, "Some Berlin Papyri Reconsidered", *ZPE* 82 (1990) 129-132 (OPENED, p. 129): BGV I 159 (216 AD), a man
  who "had fled from his village to avoid the burden of a liturgy" and came back when the prefect ordered all absentees
  home, then was extorted by an official. One case; shows flight from a liturgy, not a tax rate.
- Roger Bagnall and Clementina Caputo, "The Great Oasis in the Later Fourth Century", *GRBS* 61 (2021) 527-550 (OPENED):
  oasis sites "abandoned and others much reduced" by the end of the fourth century (p. 527); p. 534-535 says only that "if
  the total taxes in wheat at the time amounted to something like 1.3 to 1.5 artabas per aroura" the sea-transport tax
  would be a small share. That is a hypothesis, not a measured take. Causes of the decline are described as controversial.
- Search snippets only (SNIPPET ONLY): Jackier, Cope essay (Karanis surplus 81 per cent lower in the early fourth century
  than the second, several causes); Arsinoite local migration studies built on Philadelphia tax lists.

### 1.2 Abandoned land, late Roman law

- Cam Grey, "Revisiting the 'problem' of agri deserti in the Late Roman Empire", *Journal of Roman Archaeology* 20 (2007)
  362-376: paywalled, abstract unavailable. SNIPPET ONLY at best (a secondary paper says Grey defines agri deserti as
  plots that did not provide revenue to the fisc).
- Simona Tarozzi, "Riforma dello Stato e gestione della terra: la questione degli agri deserti nella prospettiva dei Codici
  tardo antichi", *MEFRA* 125-2 (2013), https://journals.openedition.org/mefra/1861. OPENED (fetch summary). It reports no
  percentage or iugera of deserted land. It cites constitutions: CTh 5.11.8 (365): three years of immunity from tribute for
  anyone who takes up deserted land; Herodian's report that Pertinax gave ten years of immunity and ownership; CTh 5.11.11
  (386): land passes to volunteer cultivators with past taxes remitted, owners given until the following May to come back;
  CTh 11.1.3 (412) and CTh 11.1.4 (337): owners must keep paying tax on land they abandoned; CTh 5.14.34 (394): owners of
  fertile land must take over unproductive land; CTh 11.1.17 (371): heirs who will not accept the tax burden must renounce
  the whole estate. Tarozzi also notes that Book 5 survives badly and is rebuilt from the Justinianic Code.
- A student paper (Philomathes, Austin Peay State University, Moens) argues the tax regime drove desertion. OPENED but not
  a reliable source; used for nothing.
- The Theodosian text itself: archive.org copy of Pharr's translation returned 401; no open full text found. NO SOURCE FOUND
  for a deserted share of land or the tax per iugerum at which owners walked away.

What this gives: the state's responses are rules a state can choose by actor-neutral levers (immunity period for taking up
abandoned land, joint liability of neighbours or owners for abandoned land, remission). Which of these a given state used is
content, not engine.

### 1.3 Medieval England

- Matt Raven, "Servile migration and seigniorial reaction in England: the serfs of Great Waltham and High Easter (Essex),
  c. 1336-1361", *Continuity and Change* 39 (2024) 101-125, doi:10.1017/S0268416024000183. OPENED.
  - p. 101-102: two routes for a serf who wanted to leave: pay chevage (an annual fee for leave), or flee and risk recapture.
  - p. 108, Table 1: only 52 servile migrants are named in the court rolls over 1291-1361 (four of them female).
  - p. 109: almost half of recorded migrants moved within nine miles and almost three quarters stayed within twenty;
    Dyer's Midlands study found 22 per cent at 10-19 miles and about 7 per cent beyond fifty. The outside option is local.
  - p. 113: in 1291 fifteen servile tenants were found living beyond the lordship without leave and none was later
    recorded paying chevage; in 1353 twelve were named absent, two later paid chevage. After 1349 "most of those who left
    without seeking the lord's permission do not appear to have been found by the lord's officials".
  - p. 117: enforcement was hard "exacerbated by competition between lords for tenants and workers, which added to the
    multiplicity of 'outside options'"; the "vast majority" of absconders were not forcibly retrieved; "the certainty of
    punishment is seemingly more effective as a deterrent than the severity of punishment".
  - This is about servile flight after the Black Death, not about a tax rate. No tallage or rent share at which flight began.
- Poll taxes of 1377 and 1381, concealment: LAMAS Special Series vol. 4, pp. 313-322, "A new roll of 4 Ric. II, 1380-1381"
  (Middlesex poll tax; Archaeology Data Service PDF). OPENED. Its footnotes give returned counts: Hertfordshire 19,975 in
  1377 and 13,296 in 1381; Cumberland 11,841 in 1377 and 4,748 in 1381; Lancaster 23,880 in 1377 and 8,371 in 1381; and
  state "It is perfectly clear that there was considerable evasion of the tax in 1381." Mine: the 1381 count is 67 per cent
  of the 1377 count in Hertfordshire, 40 per cent in Cumberland, 35 per cent in Lancaster. The tax rate per head in each
  year was not opened, and the fall mixes hiding, flight, death and miscounting, so it is an upper bound on evasion, not a
  measure of it. Search snippet only (SNIPPET ONLY): a national fall of about one third from 1377 (Oman, via a blog), a
  royal inquiry into evasion in January 1381.
- NO SOURCE FOUND: a medieval English study giving the share of rent or tallage at which tenants fled, for flight from
  heavier tax rather than from servile status.

### 1.5 Existing models

- Pickhardt and Seibold, "Income Tax Evasion Dynamics: Evidence from an Agent-based Econophysics Model", arXiv 1112.0233,
  section 2.1. OPENED. Allingham and Sandmo (1972): the payer declares X of true income W to maximise
  EU = (1-p)(W - theta X) + p(W - theta X - pi (W - X)), with p the audit probability, theta the rate on declared income,
  pi the rate on undeclared. Risk-neutral payers declare all if theta/pi < p and none if theta/pi > p; interior solutions
  need risk aversion. Extensions it names: audit probability rising with undeclared income (Yitzhaki 1987); a penalty
  proportional to unpaid tax (Yitzhaki 1974), "which ensures that the tax rate theta has no influence on the extent of tax
  evasion". Heterogeneity enters through individual risk aversion, tax morale and social interaction.
  Reading of `sim/agents/evasion.py` (mine): its penalty is proportional to the tax, so on the Yitzhaki result the rate
  would not change declared share. In the code the rate effect comes only from the convex cost of hiding
  (`HIDING_MARGINAL_COST_AT_CEILING`), which is the unsourced piece, so the rate response is a property of that cost curve.
- SNIPPET ONLY: Bloomquist (2011), agent types honest, strategic, defiant, random, about 85,000 agents calibrated on US IRS
  data; Calimani and Pellizzari, audits with heterogeneous tax morale and matchable income; Hokamp and Seibold on public
  goods feedback. All are modern income-tax compliance models with an audit rate; none is pre-modern, none was opened.
- NO SOURCE FOUND: a published agent-based simulation of peasant flight from tax or of land abandonment (historical or
  modern) that could be compared.

### 1.6 Response structure the model can implement

Who responds, through which channel, from what state:

1. Household or tenant, channel "leave": compares what it keeps here (after the harvest and poll taxes the state's officials
   reach, `tax_burden.harvest_share_taken`) with the best outside option it can reach (income elsewhere less moving cost,
   already the form of `sim/labour/market/migration.py`). Raven and Lewis both place the option locally: reach of the option
   falls with distance. The state's reach acts twice: it lowers the chance a leaver is not found (certainty of recapture),
   and it sets the liability rule below.
2. Liability rule as a state choice: individual, or a unit (village, owner group) liable for what leavers owed. Under a
   unit rule each stayer's burden is unit assessment divided by those who remain, so leaving by one raises everyone's take.
   This is the sourced loop (Lewis p. 103-106) and it is actor-neutral. Dropping the rule or cancelling the debt is the
   state's way out (Lewis p. 107).
3. Owner, channel "walk away from the holding": already built (`titles.release`, 538); late-Roman law shows the matching
   state levers (joint liability for abandoned land, immunity years for takers, remission) as choices on the same state.
4. Return: an amnesty or lower take brings a share back within a year (Lewis p. 103-104). A return rule is the same
   outside-option comparison run in the other direction, with the arrears waived.
5. Hiding: the payer chooses share hidden against the state's reach (existing `evasion.py`), with the cost of hiding derived
   from which income is visible (in kind, in coin, on the road). Unsourced: the cost curve.
6. Revolt: leave to 486.

Validation ranges (outputs the baseline ensemble should be able to produce; not inputs, not dated):

- A village hit by collective liability can lose most of its payers over a few decades (Lewis p. 105-106: village from 150 to
  45 men; Psenathre 319 to 10 over about 30 years). These are selected worst cases.
- A short shock can remove more than a tenth of adult males in a village within a few years (Lewis p. 102).
- Under an amnesty or partial forgiveness, a minority to about a third of the missing return within about a year
  (Lewis p. 103-104: 47 of 152; remaining payers' merismos falling under a tenth of the year before).
- Distance of leavers: about half within nine miles, three quarters within twenty (Raven p. 109).
- Recapture after a long absence is rare when the state's reach is low (Raven p. 113, 117).
- A head tax with evident evasion: declared counts 35 to 67 per cent of the earlier year in three counties (LAMAS
  vol. 4); upper bound on hiding plus flight plus death combined.

## 3. How to model it (structure only)

657:

- Treat flight, return and abandonment as one comparison per payer: what it keeps where it is against its best reachable
  outside option, with the option's reach falling with distance and the state's reach lowering the chance a leaver escapes.
- Make liability a state choice (individual or unit). Under a unit rule the take on a stayer is the unit's assessment over
  those who remain, so the base can collapse through a feedback. A state can cancel arrears or waive them for returners;
  the AI state's menu should value these as ways out, which is how `FISCAL_BURDEN_WEIGHT` retires.
- Keep amnesty, immunity for takers of abandoned land and remission as levers on the same state object, usable by any actor.
- Concealment stays as built, but its cost curve has to come from which income is visible, not from a typed number. Note the
  Yitzhaki point above before reading the rate response as evidence.
- Validate against the ranges in 1.6; do not fit constants to them.

660:

- Size guards from the places watched and what they face, with the staff-per-head anchors above as validation bounds and a
  separate, labelled time-per-guard. Inspectors: size from the registry (claims open, and an abandonment clock such as the
  six-month rule), not from population. Foresters: size from the tenure area a district records, not from population.
- Neither a staff-per-head figure nor a time per staffer is sourced for a whole state; the anchors are for villages and
  towns.

## 4. Recommendation

657: implement flight and return through the existing migration comparison plus a state-chosen liability rule with the unit
feedback (the one mechanism with papyrus evidence), and the state's amnesty and remission levers; keep `FISCAL_BURDEN_WEIGHT`
until the AI menu values lost base from those channels. Keep the status as partly done. State plainly that no source gives a
share of harvest at which flight starts, so the model should produce one and the report's ranges only bound it. Leave the
hiding constants labelled (`retire` 657 stands); no source for hideable share or the cost of hiding. Leave revolt to 486.

660: no new constant can be derived. Add the three staff-per-head anchors as an upper-range test for guard density (the
form the existing test takes for the capital), noting they are settled places. Leave inspector and forester sizing
labelled; search for Domergue (mining administration at Vipasca) and Young, *The Royal Forests of Medieval England*, or a
forest-eyre roll with acreage, none of which was reachable here.
