# How a trade comes into being when it has never existed

Scope: research only, no code changed. Every claim below comes from a page I opened in this session, except those labelled "snippet only" or "no source found". Wikipedia pages are used where nothing better opened; they are secondary.

## 1. Historical cases

### 1.1 Steam-engine erectors and fitters (Boulton and Watt, Soho)
Source opened: Smiles, Lives of Boulton and Watt, Project Gutenberg #52069, https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/52069/pg52069-images.html (full text fetched and searched).

- Adjacent trades. Smiles's list of early hands and Boulton's hiring of Murdock: Murdock's father "was a millwright and miller"; Boulton judged him on "a hat for himself in a lathe of his own making" and then "put upon a trial job, after which he was entered as a regular hand", engaged for two years at 15s a week. Watt himself had learned instrument making in London in about a year ("by the end of a month he was able to finish a Hadley's quadrant in better style than any apprentice in the shop"), despite the trade's seven-year apprenticeship rule. So the erector came from millwright, instrument-maker and smith stock.
- Who trained them and how. On the job, inside the firm. "Boulton and Watt contrived partially to get over the difficulty was, to confine their workmen to special classes of work, and make them as expert in them as possible. By continued practice in handling the same tools and fabricating the same articles, they thus acquired great individual proficiency." Boulton "preferred employing the humbler class of boys, whom he could train up as skilled workmen."
- Time to a competent workforce. Slow. Watt, on rotative engine orders: "we must have more men, and these we can only have by the slow process of breeding them." And: "Want of skilled workmen continued to be one of Watt's greatest difficulties. When the amount of work to be executed was comparatively small, and sufficient time was given to execute it, he was able to turn out very satisfactory workmanship; but when the orders came pouring in, new hands were necessarily taken on, who proved a constant source of anxiety". Smiles: "They could not educate mechanics fast enough, and needed all the best men for their own work."
- Early quality. New hands "were for the most part poor mechanics, very inexpert at working in metal, and greatly given to drink." Customer engine operators were "comparatively unskilled and knew nothing of the steam-engine"; a drunk engineman at the Bow engine broke valves. Erector Dudley "was pronounced incapable and a blunderer". Watt's response was to simplify the machine "so as to bring its working and repair within the capacity of the average workman". Poaching: once trained, "the difficulty was to keep them", with offers from Russia, France and German states.
- Design lesson: skill demand is a property of the technique and its tooling ("There were no slide-lathes, planing-machines, or boring-tools... Everything depended upon the individual mechanic's accuracy of hand and eye"), and can be lowered by redesign, division of labour and tools.

Source opened, thin: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soho_Foundry says production was split "to allow an extremely high degree of specialisation among workers"; it says nothing on recruiting difficulty. Snippet only: Kelly, Mokyr and Ó Gráda "Precocious Albion" and the Mokyr, Sarid and van der Beek millwright paper (cepr.org VoxEU column returned 403, so I could not read it). I do not cite their findings.

### 1.2 Arkwright's mills
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Arkwright (opened): machine credited to employee clockmaker John Kay or Thomas Highs; local labour was insufficient so he "brought in workers from outside the locality" and built cottages; "very little training was required to operate the machinery"; nearly two-thirds of 1,150 employees were children.
- https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/1835ure.asp (Ure 1835, opened): "the more skilful the workman, the more self-willed and intractable he is apt to become"; the aim is to reduce work to watchfulness and dexterity, faculties that can be "speedily brought to perfection in the young". Ure's "desultory habits" sentence: snippet only (seen via Marx quotation in a search result, not opened).
- Reading: the machine minder was made a low-skill, short-training trade by design (the skill sits in the machine); the scarce skill was the mechanic who built and kept the frames, drawn from clockmaking, millwrighting and smithing. Training time for the minder: no source found with a number.
- Learning on a machine trade, quantified (not Arkwright but cotton weaving): Bessen, https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jechis/v63y2003i01p33-64_00.html (opened): "literate workers picked up new technology faster, but local workers stayed longer"; and https://ideas.repec.org/p/roi/wpaper/0803.html (opened): most weaving productivity growth "came from improved labor quality", workers who "spent less time monitoring the looms". Abstract gives no learning duration.

### 1.3 Machinists and toolmakers (Maudslay)
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Maudslay (opened): began at the Royal Arsenal at 12 filling cartridges, then carpenter's shop, then blacksmith's forge from 15; at 18 hired by Joseph Bramah; "Many outstanding engineers trained in his workshop" (Roberts, Napier, Clement, Whitworth, Nasmyth, Field). The article says nothing of the training method.
- Nasmyth, Autobiography, Gutenberg #476, https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/476/pg476-images.html (opened and searched): Maudslay "had ceased to take pupils... the premium apprentices caused him much annoyance and irritation. They came in 'gloves;' their attendance was irregular". Nasmyth got in by showing a complete working model high-pressure engine and drawings: "Mechanical drawing is the alphabet of the engineer. Without this the workman is merely a 'hand'." He was engaged as a workman, not pupil. Skill adjacency: smith plus carpenter plus drawing.
- Machinist produced by apprenticeship in an existing machine shop, hired on demonstrated task competence. Time to competence: no source found with a number.

### 1.4 Railway enginemen
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Entwistle (opened): "Apprenticed as an engineer at the age of 11", at 16 on the footplate of Rocket's first trials; became official driver. No trainer named.
- https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Engines_and_Men/Chapter_1 (opened): Thomas West Smith started as a porter, later "cleaned and fired" The Comet; George Stephenson went farm labourer to brakeman to colliery stoker. "The chapter doesn't describe formal recruitment or training." Pattern: promotion up a cleaner, fireman, driver ladder inside the new employer; first generation from colliery and engineering stock.
- Liverpool and Manchester Railway Wikipedia page (opened): staff accidents more common than passenger accidents, drivers reprimanded for speeding. No numbers on error rate. Training time and numbers: no source found.

### 1.5 Telegraph operators
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_telegraphy (opened): "In the late 1840s, a shortage of qualified operators led to the hiring of women as well as men"; one colony's course "initially the syllabus was limited to becoming proficient in operation of the morse key"; another colony trained "by an experienced operator rather than external training"; new hires had a "three-month probationary period" under supervision. It gives no learning duration or error rate.
- Snippet only: Cooke trained railway clerks in 1837 (IET archive record); needle telegraph chosen partly for minimal operator training (IEEE Spectrum). I did not open either.
- Reading: operators drawn from clerks and the literate; trained by early operators; apprenticeship plus probation. Time and error rates: no source found.

### 1.6 Printers after Gutenberg
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Gutenberg (opened): goldsmith background, type alloy of lead, tin and antimony; Schöffer "had worked as a scribe in Paris"; the shop "may have employed many craftsmen". Training of others: not described.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printing_press (opened): "As early as 1480, there were printers active in 110 different places"; "By the end of the 15th century around 270 European cities were involved in printing"; presses were portable, so suppressed printers moved on. No training or journeyman detail.
- Reading: spread by movement of people who already hold the skill (existing-trade spread, not a new-trade case). Training time and error rates: no source found.

### 1.7 Photographers
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daguerreotype (opened): French state gave the process "free to the world" on 19 August 1839; Berry patented in Britain; Beard's studio was set up with help from Goddard; early portrait exposures were several minutes, later "between fifteen and thirty seconds". It does not describe how amateurs learned.
- Snippet only: Met Museum essay on the daguerreian age (fetch returned 429): a manual in September 1839, dozens of editions that year, painters and scientists early adopters.
- Reading: a published manual let adjacent trades (painters, chemists, instrument makers) self-train; productivity depended on tooling improvements (lens, chemistry) as much as operator skill. Training time and error rates: no source found.

### 1.8 Cross-case summary (only what the sources above support)
1. First workers came from the nearest physical-skill trades: millwright, smith, clockmaker, instrument maker, carpenter, colliery engineman, clerk, painter.
2. Teaching was by the inventor's firm or the first practitioners, on the job, with a manual only in the photograph case (snippet only).
3. Competent workforce was the binding constraint and slow ("breeding them"); firms narrowed tasks and simplified machines rather than wait.
4. Early quality was poor (Soho "poor mechanics", unskilled enginemen); trained staff were poached.
5. Redesigning technique and tooling lowered the skill needed (Watt, Arkwright), so skill demand belongs to the technique, not the trade.

## 2. Computational side

- Task-based distance. Gathmann and Schönberg, "How General is Human Capital? A Task-Based Approach", draft at https://doku.iab.de/fdz/events/2008/Gathmann.pdf (opened via pdftotext). Define each occupation by the share of workers using each task; distance is "angular separation or uncentered correlation" (cosine of the angle) of those vectors, "the cosine angle between their positions in vector space". Findings: workers "are much more likely to move to similar occupations", "the distance of actual moves... declines sharply" with experience; "the skill requirements of a baker and a cook are very similar", "switching from a banker to an unskilled construction worker would be the most distant move". Human capital is weighted by tenure in earlier occupations "inversely weighted by the distance", so skill transfers partly.
- O*NET gap method: https://www.r-bloggers.com/2026/08/identifying-skills-based-occupational-transition-pathways-with-the-onet/ (opened, a blog following Bratanova et al. 2026). Abilities, skills and knowledge each rated on level (0 to 7) and importance (1 to 5); similarity from mean absolute gaps, min-max rescaled, with a threshold of 70 for "technically viable". Secondary source.
- Revealed relatedness from labour flows: Neffke and Henning 2013, abstract page https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v34y2013i3p297-316.html (opened; paraphrase only): relatedness measured from "workers moving between industries", and firms diversify into skill-related industries far more than unrelated ones. Not usable in a world with no flows, which is why the first approach (task vectors) is needed.
- Mobility network: del Rio-Chanona et al., https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7879770 (opened). Edges are empirical transition probabilities from US survey data, "no nodes are removed or added". This is the lesson: observed networks cannot represent a brand-new node, so a simulator needs a distance computed from content, not from flows.
- Wright's law: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experience_curve_effects (opened): Wright (1936), aircraft labour time "fell by 20% with each doubling of output"; unit cost of the xth unit is C1 times x to the power log2(b), b the progress ratio.
- Arrow 1962: abstract opened at https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v29y1962i3p155-173..html ("increases in per capita income cannot be explained simply by increases in the capital-labor ratio"; knowledge should not be treated as exogenous). The model detail is from a secondary page, https://hetwebsite.net/het/essays/growth/endogenous.htm (opened): learning is a function of cumulative investment, with a learning parameter below one for diminishing returns. The Horndal works figures are snippet only (search result), not confirmed from an opened page.
- Liberty ships: P. Thompson working paper (opened as PDF, text extracted), http://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/dev/papers/9712/9712001.pdf: earlier work found "each doubling of cumulative output reduced labor hours per ship by between 12 and 24 percent" and Rapping "mean of gamma over six regressions was 0.23"; but at Calship "capital deepening and quality change... account for virtually all the increase in labor productivity", with quality measured by the "probability of a ship developing serious fractures". Capital investment accounted for a "70 percent reduction in labor requirements". Thornton and Thompson 2001, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.91.5.1350 (abstract opened): spillovers across yards were a major driver, "possibly exceeding the effect of a yard's own learning", while externalities between yards were small.
- Implications for the model: (a) the curve is per cumulative output of a technique, with diminishing returns; (b) learning is confounded with capital and quality, so do not tune it to match book productivity (CLAUDE.md 4.5, 4.1); (c) experience pooled across yards matters, so a spillover weight tied to people moving and teaching is justified.

## 3. What exists in the repo (read just enough)

- data/world/trades.json: 39 trades. Fields: family, training_years (years before productive), taught_from (a single trade string, only 5 trades have it: chemist from scribe, engineer from smith, machinist from smith, optician from glassblower, shipwright from carpenter), initially_absent (chemist, electrician, engineer, machinist, optician), literate, tool_basket, staff_resource, fatality_risk_per_year, note.
- sim/labour/labour_training.py: train_check and train create a trade. Source defaults to `trade_data.taught_from`; refuses if the source trade is absent (`source_trade_absent`); literacy caps headcount; founder hours are a flat TEACHING_HOURS_PER_PERSON (450, temporary heuristic); apprentices mature after a flat TEACHING_MATURATION_YEARS (2.0, temporary heuristic) regardless of trade; fee is TEACHING_FEE_MULTIPLIER (1.2) times the source trade's annual wage. Only the founder teaches; trainees are fully productive on maturity; no learning curve.
- Gaps against the evidence: one source per trade, a hand-picked flat time and cost, only the founder as teacher, no skill distance, no output-linked learning, no error rate, no poaching.

## 4. Proposed mechanism (no trade names in the engine)

### 4.1 Data
1. A shared list of task dimensions, as data (for example tasks.json): physical operations such as shaping hot metal, fine measurement, machine tending, reading and writing, calculation, animal handling, chemistry handling. Authored once, extendable by mods.
2. Each trade declares a task vector: weight per task dimension, summing to one. This is the Gathmann-Schönberg input and needs no flow data.
3. Each technique (tree node or recipe) declares a task demand vector and a `skill_complexity` scalar. Redesign or tooling can lower the complexity (Watt's simplified engine, Arkwright's frames, slide lathes), which makes simplification a normal technique effect rather than a special case.
4. An optional `learned_from` list on a trade, `[{"trade": ..., "distance": ...}]`, as an author override where tasks cannot capture something (the literacy requirement already exists as `literate`). If omitted the engine derives the list from task vectors. This replaces the single `taught_from` string.
5. `learning_progress_ratio` per trade family (Wright's b), with a floor and an `error_rate_at_first` and `error_rate_at_competence`, all labelled heuristics with retire references. Existing `training_years` stays as the full-apprenticeship time for a trainee starting from nothing.

### 4.2 Forming a trade when a technique needs it
- Trigger: a technique requires a trade whose headcount is zero (generalises `initially_absent`; any actor, not the founder only).
- Candidate sources: every existing trade with headcount above zero. Distance between trade a and trade b is angular separation: one minus the cosine of their task vectors (Gathmann and Schönberg). Where the new trade needs several tasks, take the best two or three sources and let each cover the task weights it holds, so a machinist can be a smith plus a draughtsman.
- Uncovered share of tasks = the weight of the new trade's tasks not held by the chosen sources.
- Training time = training_years of the new trade times (floor_fraction + (1 minus floor_fraction) times uncovered share). A near trade (baker to cook) is short; a far one (banker to construction) approaches a full apprenticeship. The sourced finding that real transfers are mostly short-distance is why the floor stays small.
- Cost: wages forgone during training (source wage times time) plus teacher time, replacing the flat 1.2 multiplier and the flat 450 hours.
- Teacher: whoever holds the knowledge, not necessarily the trade. Each actor carries knowledge of a technique (from the database or a manual) separate from practised proficiency. Teaching capacity per year is limited by teacher hours and a class size, and cohort quality is capped by the teacher's proficiency. The first cohort taught by a founder who holds knowledge but no practice starts at low proficiency and errs often (Soho's first hands, the Bow engine); later cohorts are taught by earlier graduates, so capacity grows geometrically with a delay (Watt's "breeding" and Smiles's "could not educate mechanics fast enough").
- A written manual lets adjacent trades self-train at a reduced rate and higher error rate with no teacher (daguerreotype, snippet only). Manual is just another carrier of knowledge with a lower transfer fraction.
- Poaching and attrition: trained workers are scarce, so their market wage rises and other actors can bid them away, which the existing labour market already prices (Smiles: offers from abroad).

### 4.3 Productivity along a learning curve
- Track cumulative output per technique per actor (and a pooled community figure). Time per unit = first_unit_time times cumulative output to the power log2(progress_ratio), with a floor set by physics (Wright's law). The exponent is not tuned to a historical result; the floor comes from the technique's physical minimum.
- Per-worker proficiency starts at one minus distance-based shortfall and rises with that worker's own repetitions, plus a spillover term proportional to the number of proficient co-workers and teachers present (Thornton and Thompson). Narrow task assignment (division of labour) speeds the rise (Smiles).
- Error rate (spoilage, breakdown, injury risk, defect) falls with proficiency. This is separate from speed, because the Liberty evidence shows speed gains can hide quality loss and rise with capital deepening, so productivity must not be attributed to learning alone.
- Retention: forgetting when output stops (Thompson 2007 paper title seen in a search result, snippet only; do not rely on it). Leave forgetting as a labelled heuristic, or omit.

### 4.4 Fit to the existing code
- Replace `taught_from` with derived sources; keep it as an optional override for tests.
- `train_check`: choose sources by distance instead of one name; compute hours and maturation from distance and teacher proficiency rather than the two flat constants; keep the household_room and literacy checks.
- `train`: instead of one maturation date, apprentices enter as a cohort with a proficiency that grows.
- Both flat constants and the 1.2 multiplier are already declared temporary heuristics; retiring them against this mechanism fits CLAUDE.md 4.4. Under 4.7 none of this names a trade; the task list and vectors live in data.
- Tests (CLAUDE.md section 6): a regression that creating a trade from a near source takes less time than from a far one; that a cohort taught by a graduate has higher starting proficiency than one taught by a knowledge-only founder; that cumulative output lowers unit time with a floor; that error rate falls with proficiency. All assert on structured values.

### 4.5 Open questions and evidence gaps
- Distances calibrated from task vectors need authored values; derive them from the physical operations in docs/ where possible, not from outcomes.
- Learning-curve exponents and training times for telegraphers, enginemen, printers and photographers: no source found. Do not hard-code; mark confidence D until sourced.
- Whether the chosen exponent should be per trade family or per technique: the sources support a per-industry range (12 to 24 percent per doubling in shipbuilding per the Thompson paper's summary of earlier work), not a single universal value.
- The Kelly and Mokyr millwright literature would strengthen the "upper-tail skill stock" claim; I could not open it.
