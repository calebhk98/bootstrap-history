# Bootstrap History — Yearly Campaign Log

This is a player-perspective diary. It records only information learned through the README and the game's player-facing interface. It is not derived from inspecting game files or save data.

## 1300 AD — Campaign Start

### Setup
- Civilization: England under Edward I, 1300
- Fog: ON
- Starting kit: poor_scholar
- Mortality: OFF
- Main victory goal: Transistor
- Horizon: Standard, 500 years

### What I think is happening
I am starting as a poorly funded scholar in a relatively institutionally developed medieval kingdom, but with major demographic and economic shocks ahead. I expect the game to punish narrow technological rushing if I fail to build enough economic, educational, and institutional capacity first.

### Current priorities
1. Learn the visible systems without using hidden information.
2. Build resilience before the major famine/plague period if the game gives me tools to do so.
3. Raise literacy and preserve knowledge as parallel goals, not just chase the transistor tree.
4. Establish dependable income/institutions so future projects can continue without constant survival scrambling.

### Personal goals
- Reach the Transistor victory condition under fog without using hidden tree knowledge.
- Curb epidemic mortality, ideally before or during the major plague period.
- Reach at least 20% literacy, then push toward 75% if the run remains viable.
- Build a durable scholarly/technical institution rather than relying entirely on one immortal founder.
- Industrialize enough to achieve electrical generation and interchangeable manufacturing as major milestones.
- Avoid pursuing the 'own 1,000 enslaved people' goal as a campaign objective.

### First impressions
The setup is unusually good at producing a strategic identity before the first turn. England gives me enough historical leverage that success feels plausible, but the looming crises make it feel less like a sandbox victory lap. Fog makes the run much more interesting because I have to reason from what is visible rather than optimize from a complete tech graph.

### Confidence / prediction
My current guess is that the game will be mechanically learnable but strategically difficult under fog. I expect to make inefficient choices because I do not know the tree. I think reaching some mid-tier goals should be very feasible; reaching the transistor quickly may be much harder than the setup screen's theoretical minimum suggests.

### Things I am watching for
- Whether population shocks actually change labor, prices, institutions, and project feasibility in a convincing way.
- Whether literacy behaves like a real capacity multiplier or merely a checklist stat.
- Whether institutions can carry knowledge and production independently of the player.
- Whether money, labor, social status, and state capacity interact or are mostly separate currencies.
- Whether technological prerequisites feel historically causal or just arbitrary unlock chains.

## 1300 AD — First Commitments
- Started **Ligature over cautery for haemostasis** as a deliberate cash-flow project rather than a transistor prerequisite. Cost 9,139 pence, 60 founder-hours, 0.5-year floor, 10% failure risk.
- Also took **Legal protection for physicians** because the interface presented it as a zero-cost, zero-time, zero-risk option with some downstream value.

- Ligature completed successfully during the year.
- Ended at 1301 with ~6,238 pence before opening the completed venture. Reputation rose to about 7.4; scandal appeared at 5.8, which I did not expect and need to understand.
- Opinion: the first real economic decision was easy to understand. The free institutional carryover is more questionable.

## 1301 AD — Stabilising the Practice, Starting Method

- Recurring cash flow immediately moved from negative to about +1,752 pence/year.

- Ended at 1302 with 2,930 pence and recurring surplus ~+7,928/year as the surgical practice gained custom.
- Scientific method had absorbed this year's founder work and half its money; only the calendar/payment pace remained.
- Opinion: I like that projects can be calendar-bound rather than consuming exclusive attention. That makes parallel development sensible without pretending the founder literally spends every hour staring at one experiment.

## 1302 AD — Parallel Work and First Failure
- Checked population and values. England is ~4.37M people, ~12% urban; my actual household reach is one ~15k town, so national population does not magically become my labor pool.
- Player-facing population text explicitly references `labour.py` and a placeholder density table. That is technical/developer language in a normal gameplay screen and breaks immersion/nontechnical expectations.
- Started **Horizontal loom** alongside scientific method. It cost 5,756 pence, 80 founder-hours, 0.5-year floor, 10% failure risk, and has several downstream uses plus a promising venture.
- Scientific method FAILED its first attempt. Lost 2,856 pence and 140 founder-hours. The retry failure chance fell from 20% to 15%, and 0.7 of 2.0 calendar years carried forward.
- Horizontal loom completed successfully.
- Ended 1303 at -1,679 pence cash/arrears but recurring surplus ~+12,972/year before opening the loom, so I judge the debt manageable.
- Opinion: failure-with-learning is excellent. I am much happier losing partial resources and learning from failure than watching a project simply reset.

- Opinion: this is the first decade where the simulation starts to feel more convincing than a normal tech tree. The limiting factor has shifted from cash to institutional/human capacity. That is promising.
- Concern: the score reports resilience normalized at 0.980 while the hazard screen still presents the coming famine/plague as severe and knowledge as completely unhedged. The score should explain what “resilience” actually measures so this does not look contradictory.

## 1311 AD — Arithmetic Lands; Statistics and Famine Preparation

## 1337 AD — War Begins
- Tailstock/dead-centre and cutting fluid completed.
- Recurring income rose to roughly **+58k/year**.
- The **Hundred Years War** began. The game reported trade/output at 88% of normal, softened because I already had power that did not depend on imported fuel/trade.
- Started **Roman paved road with surveying** as a wartime-local-logistics investment.
- Opinion: I like that the war interacts with my existing infrastructure instead of applying one flat universal debuff. The “local power/land/roads” logic is readable and strategically useful.

## 1338 AD — Crop Rotation Finished; Portfolio Reallocation
- **Crop rotation** and the paved road completed.
- Crop rotation could not initially open because I was only 0.2 craft-FTE short of the required supervision.
- Rather than hire another whole worker, I **mothballed the horizontal loom**, freeing 0.27 craft-FTE, then opened crop rotation.
- Opening crop rotation cost about 30,960 upfront and quoted ~116,101/year revenue against 30,960/year upkeep at maturity.
- Engineer training was still pending.
- Opinion: mothballing a weak, saturated textile concern to redeploy supervision into agriculture was one of the most satisfying portfolio decisions so far. The supervision-share model makes “staff” meaningfully allocative rather than binary.

## 1339 AD — Engineer Arrives; Economy Breaks Open
- The engineer completed training and joined staff.
- Capital was almost back to zero while recurring income surged to roughly **+112.5k/year**.
- Started **Height gauge** and **Sampling inspection** based on their names as obvious quality/precision infrastructure.
- Hired another **artisan** for operational redundancy; household staffing reached 6/6 places.
- Both height gauge and sampling inspection failed their first attempts, preserving partial progress and lowering future failure risk.
- Opinion: by this point money stopped being the primary problem. The economy can now fund serious science; labor, household capacity, and project attention are the tighter constraints.

## 1340 AD — Written Corpus and Epidemiology Guess
- Capital reached roughly **63k**, recurring net about **+149.5k/year**.
- Started **Write the corpus**. It was finally affordable but demanded 3,200 scribe-hours/year against only about 1,303 available locally, immediately making literacy/scribe supply the binding constraint.
- From visible names, guessed that **Mortality table and actuarial science**, **Case series analysis**, and **Medical statistics** were likely stepping stones toward epidemiology/clinical trials. Started all three.
- Opinion: this is exactly the kind of tech-tree reasoning I want under fog. I was not told the route; I formed a domain hypothesis from names and accepted the chance of being wrong.

## 1341 AD — Wealthy, but Founder Attention Becomes Scarce
- Capital rose to about **179k** with recurring income still around +150k/year.
- Six active projects now competed for founder-hours. The corpus, as priority #1, absorbed essentially all of my 2,000 founder-hours, leaving several tiny health/precision projects idle.
- A major fire during 1341 destroyed **54,976 pence**.
- Opinion: founder attention finally became a real bottleneck. This is good, but the UI did not proactively teach me to use `allocate` when one project starved the rest.

## 1342 AD — Manual Allocation Solves Project Starvation
- Used `portfolio` and then `help commands` to discover the **allocate** command.
- Set standing founder-hour allocations so the corpus could no longer monopolize all personal effort.
- **Case series analysis, Height gauge, Sampling inspection, and Mortality table/actuarial science** all completed during 1342.
- **Medical statistics** failed its first attempt; retry odds improved.
- Capital reached ~250k and recurring income remained ~151k/year.
- Opinion: `allocate` is a good tool once found. I want `state` or `portfolio` to suggest it automatically the first time priority starvation occurs.

## 1343 AD — Side Branches Pay Off, War Still Bites
- Corpus reached about 88% of founder-hours complete.
- Medical statistics was on retry.
- A wartime/banditry supply disruption cost **52,980 pence**.
- Capital still rose to roughly **380k**.
- Opinion: by now one-off losses that would have been catastrophic in 1310 are annoying rather than existential. The economic snowball is becoming very strong.

## 1344 AD — Public-Health Infrastructure Becomes Affordable
- **Medical statistics** completed.
- Epidemiology still remained blocked by one unheard-of prerequisite, so my mortality-table/case-series/medical-statistics hypothesis was incomplete.
- Started **Roman sewer with gravity flow** and **Roman aqueduct with inverted siphons** ahead of the Black Death.
- Capital ~477k; recurring income ~164k/year.
- Opinion: this was the point where the game changed from “can I afford sanitation?” to “can I build the right sanitation in time?” That is a much more interesting problem.

## 1345 AD — Sanitation Stack Expands
- Roman aqueduct and Roman sewer completed by the end of the year; both required explicit `open` to become operational.
- **Flush toilet with S-bend water trap** also completed.
- Started **Sewer separated from stormwater drain**.
- Browsing disease-related visible names also showed that epidemiology was still not startable.
- Opinion: I like the distinction between building/knowing infrastructure and actually operating it. A closed sewer does not magically protect anyone.

## 1346 AD — Clean-Water Chain Becomes Explicit
- Opened the Roman sewer and aqueduct, adding recurring upkeep with no direct revenue.
- Risk screen showed Black Death staff loss down from 40% to **34%**, but did not yet count the system as “clean water.”
- The hazard screen explicitly revealed **Sand filtration** as the next filtered-water step; started it immediately.
- Hundred Years War output penalty was shown as substantially softened by self-feeding agriculture, local power, and own roads.
- Opinion: this is some of the game's best causal modeling so far. Aqueduct + sewer alone are not treated as purified drinking water.

## 1347 AD — Sanitation Ready Just in Time
- **Stormwater separation** and **Sand filtration** completed during the year.
- Started **Boolean algebra — logic and switching equivalence** as a small, name-obvious modern-foundation project while the large infrastructure work waited on calendar time.
- Capital passed **600k** with roughly +169k/year recurring.
- Opinion: the economy is now easy enough that I can pursue resilience and science simultaneously. Human capital and hidden institutional gates are much harder than money.

## 1348 AD — Black Death Begins; Filtered Water Goes Live
- The Black Death hazard became active.
- Opened **Sand filtration** before resolving the year's plague check.
- Risk immediately improved: staff loss multiplier dropped to 65% of baseline, with projected per-wave household loss down to **29%** (from 40% earlier). The screen explicitly credited **filtered water**, alongside crop/fodder resilience.
- No household plague wave landed in the 1348 resolution.
- Boolean algebra completed.
- Opinion: this was a satisfying payoff for planning years ahead. The game gave a clear causal line from aqueduct/sewer to filtration to lower epidemic losses.

## 1349 AD — Corpus Completes; Plague Finally Lands
- Started **Binary arithmetic — base-2 number system** after Boolean algebra exposed it.
- The **written corpus completed**.
- The Black Death finally hit during 1349:
  - household staff **-17%**;
  - England's population **-24%**;
  - the game stated country-level public-health diffusion held this below the **45%** loss that would otherwise have occurred (45% softer).
- The event message also said some agricultural mitigation had **lapsed**, which needs clearer explanation because those concerns were still conceptually part of my resilience plan.
- The corpus completion message said it was **closed / not operating** and “not in effect until open,” yet at the 1350 state/risk screens the game already credited it as a knowledge hedge. This is a real consistency/clarity issue.
- Opinion: the plague event felt huge without being run-ending. The fact that preparation materially reduced losses made the preceding decade feel consequential rather than cosmetic.

## 1350 AD — Fourth Checkpoint
- Stopped exactly at the start of 1350 without making any 1350 gameplay decisions.
- Capital: **916,108 pence**.
- Recurring net: about **+168,555/year**.
- Staff: **4.9 FTE** after plague attrition; household capacity 6.0.
- England population estimate: **3,255,529**, 12% urban; local operating town ~14,378.
- Built personally: **45 technologies**, 204 granted for free; only **19 confirmed on the transistor road**. I have spent this era heavily on resilience/institutions rather than shortest-path progress.
- General literacy remains about **6%**; provisional institutional score is still **0**.
- Provisional resilience score is ~0.998 normalized despite an active Black Death still projecting 29% loss per wave; the meaning of this score remains opaque.
- Black Death has **one annual check remaining**, about a 32% chance of another wave at current protection.
- Knowledge risk screen says `corpus_written` now reduces sack-loss chance from 80% to 45% and loss fraction from 40% to 22%, despite the corpus still being closed.
- Binary arithmetic is active with one calendar/funding year remaining.
- Opinion: by 1350 the economy is no longer difficult. The real game is now human capital, institutions, preservation, and finding productive routes through a huge fogged tree. I am economically ahead of where I expected, technologically much less direct than a speedrunner, and still enjoying the campaign.

## 1351–1375 campaign diary

Binary arithmetic and the respectable social identity finished. My engineer disappeared immediately, putting sewage-separation work on an abandonment clock. I expanded household housing and began training a replacement engineer. The big lesson was that technical progress remains fragile when a whole profession is represented by one person.

### 1352
Local patronage finished and I opened it, then began Newtonian mechanics. I also started logarithm tables and quadratic work and immediately discovered the scale of the scribe bottleneck: about 40,000 hours/year of project demand against roughly 1,260 initially available. Hiring two scribes raised usable supply to about 5,260 hours/year, proving that staffing directly changes research throughput rather than serving as decoration.

### 1353
A carpenter left, so I replaced him before allowing operating concerns to become brittle. Broader browsing exposed the First Workshop and Laboratory, which I started rather than continuing to interrogate case hardening directly. By year-end sewage separation, Newtonian mechanics, and the first workshop/laboratory all completed. This was a major conceptual milestone: what had looked like a mysterious metallurgy prerequisite turned out to require an actual workshop/laboratory culture.

Case hardening and refractory fireclay became startable; I began both. I also found the institutional chain toward education by names: legal standing/citizenship appeared to lead toward a licensed collegium, so I started the expensive citizenship project despite it not looking like the fastest transistor move.

### 1355
Case hardening completed and was confirmed on the transistor road. I hired another artisan so it could actually operate. Three-plate precision surfaces and crank/conrod work became visible; I started both. This year reinforced that a completed technique is not the same thing as industrial capacity—the supervision to operate it has to come from somewhere.

### 1356
Crank/conrod and calculus-limit work completed. Fireclay failed its first attempt but retained partial progress and improved retry odds. I started the treadle lathe. The retry system continues to feel punishing enough to matter without turning bad RNG into a reset button.

### 1357
Citizenship, treadle lathe, three-plate precision, and fireclay completed. Citizenship finally made the licensed collegium visible. I hired more artisans and opened the precision/fireclay concerns, then began the collegium. ### 1358
The licensed collegium failed its first attempt, while 1100°C heat capability completed. I followed the visible precision names toward 0.1 mm tolerances and the analytical balance chain. Chemistry still looked blocked less by remembered knowledge than by the absence of reproducible instruments, which is exactly the right kind of realism for the premise.

### 1359
The licensed collegium and high-temperature capability completed; I lost an artisan and an engineer. I opened the collegium. A university became visible as a direct institutional follow-on, so I started it. I also started cementation steel, taking on a large commitment because it looked like the bridge toward analytical chemistry.

### 1360
Cementation steel was badly throttled by charcoal availability. The game explicitly told me charcoal is grown through coppice and estimated how much forest would cover the shortage; I bought my first hectare. The 0.1 mm tolerance project failed again. For the first time an upstream physical material supply, not money or abstract research points, was dictating industrial speed.

### 1361
Logarithms completed; charcoal was still short, so I bought another hectare of coppice. I began training two chemists. The university finished, although I also lost a scholar. Opening the university raised the reachable scholar ceiling, confirming that institutions alter the labor market rather than merely add score.

### 1362
With the university operating I hired more scholars and began electromagnetic theory. Cementation steel and quadratic equations finished. The analytical balance became startable and I began it despite its substantial labor/instrument burden. On the public-health side I started vital registration and a case-control study.

### 1363
Case-control study completed and the two chemists finished training. I added an engineer and began chlorination and activated-sludge sewage treatment. Medicine was becoming less about isolated cures and more about measurement, records, and public infrastructure.

### 1364
Electromagnetic theory completed and counted toward the transistor. The analytical balance failed its first attempt, preserving partial progress with better retry odds. I kept the retry going. Electricity was conceptually visible but most practical apparatus still depended on metallurgy or hidden engineering prerequisites.

### 1365
Water chlorination and vital registration completed. I hired additional artisans so chlorination could operate. Vital registration exposed a notifiable-disease system, which I started along with a cohort study. This felt like a plausible progression from anecdotes toward population medicine.

### 1366
The analytical balance finally completed and counted toward the transistor route. I lost a scholar. The balance was a major realism milestone: knowing chemistry from memory was finally becoming operational because I could measure small quantities reproducibly.

### 1367
Activated sludge, cohort study, and the notifiable-disease system completed. I opened the analytical balance. Despite mortality tables, case series, cohort work, notification, and medical statistics, epidemiology still remained one unknown concept away; my reasoning was directionally useful without becoming a free hidden-tree solver.

### 1368

### 1370
I bought a fourth hectare of coppice. I opened lens grinding, hired another artisan when potash/soda lacked supervision, and opened potash/soda too. An artisan and my remaining scribe later left. ### 1371
I bought a fifth hectare of coppice.
