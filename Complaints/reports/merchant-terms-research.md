## 2. MONOPOLY_MARKUP_SHARE: derive from the destination's residual demand, then delete the constant

**Derivation.** A merchant chooses the quantity (or reservation price) that maximises `(price_at_destination(quantity) - cost_per_unit) * quantity`, where `cost_per_unit` is purchase price plus freight, interest over the cycle, loss and agents. `price_at_destination(quantity)` is the destination market's inverse residual demand: buyers' schedule minus supply already on offer from rivals and local makers, the dominant-firm-with-fringe rule of `innovator-pricing-research.md` section 2.2. The markup is an outcome, not an input. For a lone merchant facing locally constant elasticity it reduces to the textbook inverse-elasticity rule that the constant stands in for; with several merchants choosing quantities it falls toward cost (Cournot), which is what `competition_markup_share` imitates by 1 over the number. Both limits come out of the same maximiser, so neither the constant nor the 1-over-n form is needed.

**State it reads.** The destination market's book (buyers' schedule and offered reservations, the `market_curves.summarize` rows), the merchants' own cost build-up, and rival merchants' committed cargo on the route (the `RouteShares` idea in `merchants_shares.py`, which already tracks what rivals have bid into a destination). For the engine layer, the same book must exist for the foreign market, which is Complaint 382 step 4 and 407: until a partner economy has its own demand schedule, the elasticity can only be read from the home market rescaled, and a label must say so.

**Why it is not just the pricing build.** The pricing research asks for a seller who knows the book. A merchant is a seller at the destination and a buyer at the origin, so the merchant's maximiser needs both books: residual demand at the destination and the origin's supply schedule (what its own purchases do to the price it pays, monopsony). The pricing stage 1 function (residual demand from a book) serves the first; the second is the mirror, a residual supply function. Ask the pricing build to expose it symmetrically.

The Genoese 1156 example is not in that article and stays [snippet] from a search result]. It shows how a venture's profit divides between capital and labour; it does not give the market markup.

**Relationship tests.** (1) With one merchant and a steep residual demand the markup share equals the inverse elasticity at the optimum within solver tolerance. (2) The markup falls monotonically as equal merchants are added and approaches zero margin over cost as their number grows. (3) A flatter destination demand (more substitutes) lowers the markup at fixed merchants. (4) Raising the origin's supply elasticity raises the price merchants pay less than a steeper supply would (monopsony). (5) Merchant profit over capital tends toward the interest rate where entry is free (relationship to the Venetian moderate-profit case), above it where a charter or a closed sea limits entry.

## 3. AGENTS_PER_CARRIER: a house's staffing from what it must do

**Derivation.** A house needs a person at each end for as long as cargo is bought, loaded, received and sold; the hours are the cargo's handling and selling time, not a headcount per ship. Staffing then follows from (a) tonnes handled per year at each end, (b) the time a tonne takes to buy and load, and to sell into demand (section 5), and (c) the span of control a principal can supervise by letter. Agents' cost per tonne is hours per tonne times the labour market's merchant wage, which is what the code already does for the wage; the change is replacing the "two per carrier" count by hours per tonne that fall out of the selling-time model. A house with several carriers spreads fixed presence (a factor resident at each port, who must be there however many ships call) over more tonnes, so cost per tonne falls with the house's volume at a port: a fixed-plus-variable form where the fixed part is one resident agent per port served, the variable part is handling hours per tonne.

**State it reads.** Tonnes per year per port from the freight lift ledger (`foreign_lift_capacity_tonnes`, and the domestic flow ledger of `freight-remains-research.md` section 4); the merchant wage quote; the number of ports a house serves (route legs); the selling time of section 5.

**Sourced figures.**

So a Roman house's agents were largely the ship's own master, in line with a venture carried by one principal plus a delegate rather than resident factors at both ends.
- Geniza merchants organised through partnerships, usually between co-religionists and often reinforced by kin, investing capital and engaging agents from one end of the known world to the other [read, publisher's description of S. The agent was typically a partner or relative resident at the far port [recalled; the description above does not say it], which is the "fixed presence per port" term.
- The Datini company expanded from Florence and Prato to branch offices (fondaci) in Pisa, Genoa, Avignon, Barcelona, Valencia and Palma, organised as a group of partnerships in the manner of a holding company, in 1383 [snippet: search summaries; the page https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Datini was opened and says only that he implemented "the first partnership system in business in 1383" and that 500 account books were found in 1870, with no branch list; the proper source is Padgett and McLean, 2006, "Organizational Invention and Elite Transformation", not opened]. (3) A route of one port at each end costs at least two residents' time per year regardless of how few carriers sail. (4) With the labour market's merchant wage doubled, cost per tonne doubles at fixed hours.

## 4. One merchant per carrier, and ships owned, chartered and shared

**Derivation.** Make ownership of a carrier a set of shares and the merchant a separate actor that charters freight. Three roles are separate in the sources: the owner (receives freight or hire), the operator (master, runs the voyage), and the shipper (owns the cargo, pays freight). A house is a merchant holding cargo in several carriers and shares in several carriers. Spreading is a choice: a merchant maximising expected return less a risk penalty splits capital across ships so one loss does not ruin it, limited by the fixed cost of each additional consignment and by the minimum shipment that an agent can handle. Risk pricing is the capital-markets insurance stage (below).

**State it reads.** Carrier ownership records (shares by actor), the hull loss rate (`HULL_LOSS_PER_THOUSAND_KM` over the sea legs, already used in `_route_cargo_loss_share`), freight rates per tonne, the capital market's premium for risk.

**Sourced figures.**
- Hanse merchants in the fifteenth century often owned more than one ship or held a share in several ships through partnerships; large vessels were financed by co-ownership [snippet: search summary of a Polish journal article on Hanse ship co-ownership, https://wnus.usz.edu.pl/sm/file/article/download/9508.pdf, which returned 503 and was not opened]. Partenreederei: part-owners share proceeds and debts proportionally, with unlimited liability, dating to the Middle Ages and related to the Dutch partenrederij [read, confirmed: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partenreederei].
- Genoa: a notarial deed of 17 July 1248 records one agent buying, for one principal, a sixth part of the ship Saint Leonard and a thirty-third part of the ship Saint Agnes, and the introduction says shares divided the risk of loss [read: https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/1248pship3.asp; the Marseilles comparison was not checked, and the prices were not usable]. The deed is itself a case of one merchant holding shares in two ships. The Venetian measure of galley cargo space in carats rented to individual merchants appears in the Wikipedia muda article [read, confirmed: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muda_(convoy)], where the Senate convened the auction and each patron sublet space by the carat; the article does not use "carati" for ship shares.
- Song and Yuan China: investors usually divided their investment among many ships, and each ship had many investors; classes of participant included shipowners, shippers, charterers, financiers and small merchants [snippet: search summary of Clark and Schottenhammer; Schottenhammer, 2015, Crossroads 11, https://www.ostasien-verlag.de/zeitschriften/crossroads/cr/pdf/CR_11_2015_001-030_Schottenhammer.pdf, was opened and does not contain the statement].
- Rome: the owner was often also the trader; the page says a second-century rule gave relief to those who owned one vessel of 50,000 modii capacity or several ships of that combined capacity who supplied Rome, and an edict of Claudius gave Latins citizenship for building a ship of at least 10,000 modii that carried grain to Rome for six years, showing both single-ship owners and small fleets existed [read: romanports shipowners page above; the earlier "five or more mid-size vessels" wording is not on the page and is removed]. Roman-law ship-owner associations (societates) financed their own winter expeditions [read: https://romanports.org/en/articles/human-interest/156-winter-shipping.html].

**Relationship tests.** (1) With a positive hull loss rate and a risk penalty, the optimal number of ships a merchant spreads over rises with its capital and falls with the fixed cost per consignment. (2) The ruin probability of a merchant falls as it spreads at equal expected return. (3) Freight paid equals the carrier owners' revenue, conserved to the unit. (4) A carrier with many part-owners and one operator pays each part-owner its share of net hire; no engine code names a house.

## 5. The wait: time to sell into demand, and a sailing season

**Derivation.** Cargo money is tied up from purchase to sale, and the cycle has four parts: waiting for a sailing at the origin, the voyage, waiting for a market at the destination, and selling the cargo into demand. Today only the first and second exist. Two additions:
1. **Sailing season from geography.** A route's usable months follow from the winds and seas on its legs: a closed sea or a monsoon window. Sailings per year then equal the carriers' round trips that fit in the open window; a cargo bought outside the window waits for the next one. A window comes from the leg's climate data (prevailing wind reversal for monsoon legs, winter gale frequency for the Mediterranean), not authored dates (CLAUDE.md 4.1). `sim/geography/` owns the climate; the route's legs would carry an open-fraction-of-year and an opening phase.
2. **Selling time from demand.** Selling a cargo of size `cargo_tonnes` into a market whose usual yearly volume is `market_tonnes_per_year` takes about `cargo_tonnes / (share_of_market * market_tonnes_per_year)` of a year if the merchant will not depress the price. With the residual-demand rule of section 2 the merchant instead chooses a selling rate: faster sale lowers the price, slower sale costs interest and spoilage. The optimal sale period is an output of the same maximiser with an interest term, and a perishable good shortens it. This unifies the markup and the wait: slow selling is how a lone merchant holds price.

**State it reads.** Leg climate and season data in `sim/geography/`; the destination market's expected yearly volume (`expected_volumes` in the agent economy; `market_memory`); the interest rate (`market_rate`); the good's spoilage rate (`cargo_cost.spoilage_rates`).

**Sourced figures.**
- Mediterranean in Roman times: Vegetius, Epitoma rei militaris 4.39, gives a safe season, an uncertain shoulder and a closed sea [read: https://www.romanports.org/en/articles/human-interest/156-winter-shipping.html, which quotes the Roman dates: safe from the sixth day before the Kalends of June to the rising of Arcturus, uncertain until the third day before the Ides of November, closed from then to the sixth day before the Ides of March]. The two earlier summaries do not disagree: both are Vegetius with Roman dates converted differently. Converted by counting days inclusively, the dates are 27 May, 14 September (Arcturus, eighteenth day before the Kalends of October), 11 November and 10 March, which a search summary of the Latin also gave [snippet; my own calendar arithmetic agrees; the Latin text itself was not opened, thelatinlibrary.com returned 404]. The romanports page's own conversions (6 June to 18 October, and the earlier 13 November to 6 March) are wrong. Casson is quoted there for normal activity "in the summer and a few weeks before and after", and the page says official shipping stopped from late October to early April. Use only the relationship: a closed window of several months, with off-season sailing as a risk taken by private merchants when scarcity raised prices. The Wikipedia article on mare clausum gives November to March for the Roman period and adds that bans "were probably never enforced" [read: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mare_clausum]. Large grain ships between Ostia and Alexandria took about 18 to 25 days with favourable winds and 50 to 60 days against [read: romanports page].
- Indian Ocean: Geniza ships sailed from Aden to India in late summer or autumn soon after the ships from Egypt arrived, and returned between winter and April [snippet: search summary of Margariti, Aden and the Indian Ocean Trade, UNC Press 2007; the review page returned 404 and the Cambridge and Archaeopress pages were paywalled]. For the Roman side the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (Schoff 1912 translation, https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/ancient/periplus.asp) says ships bound from Egypt for the Muziris market sail "about the month of July, that is Epiphi" (section 49) and that the wind from the ocean sets in on the Indian shore when the Etesian winds blow with us (section 57) [read]. Relationship: one round trip a year, fixed by the monsoon, so the sailings per carrier per year are at most one however fast the ship.
- Venetian mude: ships usually left Venice in spring and returned in autumn, one muda a year per route; the Aigues-Mortes muda stopped for eighteen days at its destination, and the contractors' monopoly on listed goods lasted two months after departure [read, confirmed: Muda page]. Relationship: a destination stay of weeks, so selling time is weeks to a season for a convoy cargo, not years.
- Champagne fairs: six fairs a year in a circuit, each opening with a settling-in week and then days for cloth, leather and weighed goods, with a four-day final settlement of accounts [read: Champagne fairs page; its two statements on a fair's length conflict (two to three weeks against more than six weeks), so the length is not used; six fairs, an eight-day setup and a four-day final settlement are on the page]. Relationship: a fixed calendar of selling windows, with settlement only at set dates; a merchant that misses the fair waits for the next.

**Relationship tests.** (1) With the sailing window halved at fixed carriers, the expected wait doubles (the wait is the closed fraction plus the round trip gap). (2) Selling time rises with cargo over market volume and falls with the number of competing sellers. (3) A good with a higher spoilage rate shows a shorter optimal sale period and a smaller cargo. (4) A route whose legs have no closed season has no season term (the code today). (5) Money tied up is voyage plus waits plus selling time; capital needed for a flow rises with each, in proportion.

## 6. The class's capital and lenders' room

**Derivation.** Replace `merchants * modest-merchant kit * MERCHANT_DENSITY` by the merchants' own balance sheets: each merchant actor has cash and stock; the class capital is their sum. Merchant numbers come from the labour market's trade allocation (people choosing the trade because its return beats the alternative), not a density. The initial capital is an initial condition per civilisation (allowed by CLAUDE.md 4.1) taken from data the start file already carries, such as the class's share of wealth, while later capital is accumulated or lost by the merchants' own results. Borrowing goes through the capital market: a merchant's loan request is a claim in the same pool as every other borrower's, so lenders' room is read after merchants' loans are added. In the engine today `market_credit_room` subtracts "others" from capacity but merchant borrowing in the foreign trade code is not booked as a loan, so the room is overstated; the fix is booking each flow's financing as a claim (the capital-markets stage 0 claim kind) so the pool sees it.

**State it reads.** Merchant actors' purses and stocks (the agent economy already has `Merchant.capital_base`, and `merchants_credit.credit_request` sized under `MERCHANT_LEVERAGE_LIMIT`), `record.loans`, `record.capacity` and the credit market's answer (`economy.agent_credit_room`), and the capital market's institutions (bank, equity, insurance, in `capital-markets-build-research.md` section 5).

**Sourced figures.**
- Genoese commenda and Venetian colleganza: the unilateral commenda put all capital with the investor; the bilateral form two thirds from the investor and one third from the traveller [read: Commenda page]. First documented Venetian colleganza 1073, evidence of use from the tenth century [read: same]. So merchants' own capital was a minority of what they traded with, the rest coming from investors who shared profit and loss; `BORROWING_PER_OWN_CAPITAL` of two matches the bilateral split of capital, but the investor's return was a profit share, not interest.
- Roman sea loan: the Muziris papyrus (P. Vindob. G 40822, mid-second century) preserves on its recto part of an agreement on carrying goods from the Red Sea port across the desert to Koptos and down the Nile to Alexandria, with a reference to a loan "according to Muziris", a maritime loan that financed the goods; the verso values the cargo for customs duty [read: L. Casson, 1990, "New Light on Maritime Loans: P. Vindob G 40822", Zeitschrift fuer Papyrologie und Epigraphik 84, pp. 195-206, https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/IndOc-Gulf/MuzirisPapyrus-Casson1990.pdf, introduction and p. 205 note 29]. The papyrus gives no loan rate or term in the preserved text, so none is used. Casson's reading of the repayment rule is that the debtor pays on the date fixed in the Muziris loan contracts and, if he does not, the creditor takes the pledged goods; as the ship's loss ended the security, the loan was repayable only if the ship arrived (a bottomry shape; Casson p. 206) [read]. The Arabarchs levied about a four-hundredth of the goods at the Red Sea port and the customs at Alexandria was 25 percent [read: Casson, notes on lines 10 and 13]. The cargo value is resolved: the papyrus is in drachmae, the six parcels recorded on the verso being worth 1,154 talents and 2,852 drachmae (about 6.93 million drachmae, "just short of 1155 talents", Casson note 29), which F. De Romanis reads as the value of about three quarters of the cargo, almost 7,000,000 drachmae, with the whole cargo worth almost 10,000,000 drachmae and as much as 540 tons of pepper [read: F. De Romanis, 2017, Topoi Orient-Occident Supplement 15, section "One ship, several merchants", https://art.torvergata.it/bitstream/2108/192383/2/DeRomanis_Topoi-Suppl15.pdf]. The earlier "almost 10,000,000 denarii" is a unit slip for drachmae; Wikipedia gives "around 9 million sesterces" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muziris) [read], which agrees if a drachma equals a sesterce, as one denarius equals four sesterces and a tetradrachm [recalled, not checked in the sources]. De Romanis also argues the lender's support was limited and the borrower had means of his own [read]. The Arabarchs' financial support to merchants is not established by the pages opened and stays [snippet: https://rmblf.be/2020/06/06/publication-federico-de-romanis-the-indo-roman-pepper-trade-and-the-muziris-papyrus/].
- Medici: each branch separately capitalised with the Florentine partnership holding the lion's share and a local manager a minority (the Martelli example is 2,000 of 14,000 ducats) [read, see section 3: Medici Bank page]. Pattern: capital pooled by a head house and lent to a branch, not drawn from the manager alone.
- EIC separate stock: the subscribers individually bore a voyage's cost and took its whole profit; from 1612 the voyages were conducted on the joint stock system [read: 1911 Britannica, "East India Company"; the claim that each fleet kept separate accounts is not on the page and stays [snippet]]. Capital was raised per venture before it was raised for a standing fleet.

**Relationship tests.** (1) Total loans in the pool equal the sum of borrowers' debts including merchants'; lenders' room falls as merchants borrow. (2) Merchants' capital after a lost cargo falls by the loss less insurance; a class that lost many cargoes borrows less next year. (3) Capital rises with profitable trade and the number of merchants responds through the labour market with a lag. (4) No capital figure depends on a density constant.

## 7. Retained earnings at the households' saving share

**Derivation.** A merchant's earnings are its owner's income; what is kept is the owner's saving decision, made by the same saving rule as any household's, from the owner's own income, wealth and expected returns, not a constant (and in particular not the households' `SAVING_SHARE_OF_SURPLUS` applied to the markup). A merchant in the agent economy already does a version of this: `close_year` pays the owner a share of profit (`MERCHANT_PROFIT_PAYOUT_SHARE`, a heuristic) and keeps the rest as working capital. The derivation is to use the owner's household record: the payout is the owner's income, and the owner's consumption and saving come from the household layer; reinvestment is the owner lending or staking money, which happens only if the return on trade beats the owner's other uses (loans, land). Reinvestment then responds to the interest rate.

**State it reads.** The owner's household income and wealth, the capital market's rate, the route's expected return.

**Sourced figures.** Documented savings behaviour of merchant houses is thin in what was found. The Medici managers' larger profit share than their equity gave them [read: Medici Bank page, see section 3] and the Datini partnership form [snippet] show profit shares fixed by contract and capital left in the partnership for its term, which is a retention rule set by the partnership deed, not the owner's budget. Treat retention as a contract term (the partnership's duration and the withdrawal rules) as well as a saving rule.

**Relationship tests.** (1) The share of profit retained rises with the expected return on trade relative to the owner's alternative, and with the partnership's lock-in. (2) A merchant whose owner is poorer pays out more. (3) One owner's household income conserves: payout plus retained equals profit.

## 10. Open questions

1. Two merchant implementations exist (engine foreign trade and the agent economy's `merchants.py`). Is the target one actor type, with foreign trade as the agent economy's merchants reaching foreign market areas? That removes duplicate heuristics (the two hold different margin, share and borrowing constants) but depends on 382's partner economies. Owner decision.
2. The markup maximiser makes a lone merchant restrain sales. Should a staple route allow this (as Question 2 of the pricing report asks of producers), or should a staple's destination demand floor stop it?
3. Where does the season live: on route legs from climate (geography package), or on carriers as a capability (a ship type that can sail in a closed sea)? Both are plausible; the second makes ships a technology matter (the winter exception in Rome was risk, not impossibility).
4. How does a merchant's time-to-sell relate to the fair or convoy calendar when sale dates are fixed by an institution? A fair is a market-clearing date; the yearly step has no within-year clock. Does the foreign market clear more than once a year?
5. Charters and monopolies (Venetian galley auctions, EIC): is a legal monopoly a rule an actor (a state) can create, with merchants bidding for it? The auction price would then capture the markup rent, which is a relationship worth testing but needs a state mechanism.
6. The commenda's profit split is contract-defined: should merchants' profit sharing with investors be a term of a loan-like claim (equity stage 5 of the capital-markets plan) or a share of venture profit with no fixed interest?
7. Calibration: all figures here are checks on relationships, not targets. Which documented voyage accounts will be used to check the order of magnitude of the markup, and who verifies them as read?

## 11. Verification pass: voyage accounts and changes

Documented voyage and house accounts found, with figures and sources (each is a check on an order of magnitude, not a target):

1. **Hermapollon, Muziris papyrus, mid-second century.** Verso cargo value 1,154 talents and 2,852 drachmae for the recorded parcels (about 6.93 million drachmae); De Romanis: that is about three quarters of the cargo, the whole almost 10 million drachmae; customs duty 25 percent at Alexandria (about 1.7 million drachmae on 6.93 million, my arithmetic) plus about one four-hundredth at the Red Sea port; a maritime loan secured on the goods, rate and term not preserved. Sources: Casson 1990 (ZPE 84, pp. 195-206, notes 10, 13, 29), De Romanis 2017 (links in section 6) [read].
2. **Second Dutch expedition (Van Neck), 1598 to 1600.** Nearly 800,000 guilders raised, eight ships out from Texel on 1 May 1598, Van Neck's part returning in July 1599 with nearly 450 tonnes of pepper and cloves and half a ship of nutmeg, mace and cinnamon, "netting a 400% profit for its backers" [read: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Dutch_Expedition_to_the_East_Indies]. A search summary also gave 265 percent after deposits; that figure is not on the page and is [disputed] (unresolved: it may be net of capital returned).
3. **First Dutch expedition (Houtman), 1595 to 1597.** 290,000 guilders, four ships, 248 men of whom 94 completed the voyage, a small cargo (245 bags of pepper, 41 tonnes of nutmeg, 30 bales of mace) and, because spice prices had risen, still profitable [read: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Dutch_Expedition_to_the_East_Indies]. A search summary called it a commercial failure; the article says profitable, no total given, so this is [disputed].
4. **English East India Company first voyage, 1601 to 1603.** Five ships of about 1,560 tons, the flagship bought for 3,700 pounds, away from 13 February 1601 to 11 September 1603, with plunder from a captured carrack valued at 132,000 rials; 95 percent profit by 1609, about 8 percent a year [read: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1601_East_India_Company_Voyage]. Capital for the first voyage (a search result said 68,373 pounds) was not confirmed; the company's original capital was 72,000 pounds from 125 shareholders [read: Britannica 1911].
5. **Pegolotti, road to Cathay, about 1340.** A merchant with a dragoman and two servants carrying goods worth 25,000 gold florins spends 60 to 80 sommi of silver to reach Cathay, and a sommo is worth five gold florins, so 300 to 400 florins, about 1.2 to 1.6 percent of the goods (my arithmetic); the way back costs about five sommi per pack animal [read: Pegolotti, Pratica della mercatura, in Yule's translation, https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/texts/pegol.html]. A cost account with no sale price.
6. **Veckinchusen's Venetian company, 1402 to 1417.** Capital proposed to rise from 5,000 to 11,000 marks Lubisch; in April 1411 goods bought in Venice worth about 70,000 ducats were offset by Hanse goods worth about 53,000 ducats; the company ended with the 1417 Venice trade ban [read, Wikipedia summary: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hildebrand_Veckinchusen]. A loss-side account, useful against survivorship.
7. **Soranzo fraterna ledger, 1406 to 1434.** Average profit on the firm's commercial operations 9.64 or 9.98 percent [read, abstract: Ryabova 2022, link in section 2].
