# How a trade spreads to a place that lacks it, and how far a scarce trade's wage rises (v2, sourced)

Rule used: each claim below carries a source line. "Opened" means I fetched and read the text this session (PDFs converted to text and searched). Text copies are in /tmp/claude-0/dl (not part of the repo). A claim with no opened source is listed in section 4 and is not stated as fact. No code was edited.

## 1. How an existing trade reached a place that lacked it

### 1.1 Artisan migration
- Claim: before industry, new techniques crossed regions mostly by artisans moving, as permanent emigration of masters and temporary migration of journeymen.
  Quote (Epstein 1998, p. 702): "In preindustrial economies, technological cross-fertilization occurred overwhelmingly through artisan migration." and "Technological transfer took place through the permanent emigration of master artisans and the temporary migration of journeymen."
  Source: https://bollettinoadapt.it/wp-content/uploads/epstein1998.pdf (opened)
- Claim: rulers competed for displaced craftsmen; guild opposition to alien craftsmen was weak.
  Quote (Epstein p. 702): "opposition seems to have been neither frequent nor very effective. Competition between states fostered technological diffusion." Examples given: Huguenots to Geneva and England; "the wholesale transfer of artisan skills from Brabant to the Netherlands after the sacking of Antwerp in 1585".
  Source: same Epstein PDF (opened)
- Claim: the inducement could be a large wage multiple.
  Quote (Epstein p. 702): "artisans were lured from the most technologically advanced cities with financial and legal inducements and, if necessary, protection from guild obstruction; in this way a Murano glassmaker was brought to England in the 1630s by paying him five to ten times his earnings in Venice." Footnote 71 cites Rapp, Industry, p. 109 fn. 6. This is one case, not a typical rate.
  Source: same Epstein PDF (opened)
- Claim: emigration bans were hard to enforce, and migrants were usually absorbed into the host guild.
  Quote (Epstein p. 702-703): "weak administrations and state competition made restrictions hard to enforce"; "most artisan migrants ended up being incorporated in another guild." Reason given: "only other trained workers could interpret the new information effectively", and the host guild "often saw integration as a way of controlling alien competitors".
  Source: same Epstein PDF (opened)
- Claim: journeyman tramping was larger in scale than permanent migration, and the most technical sectors had the most mobile labour.
  Quote (Epstein p. 703): "the greater scale and regularity of journeyman tramping compared with permanent artisan migration suggests that its effects may have been proportionally stronger"; "the most technically advanced sectors (mining, shipbuilding, building, luxury textile production and printing) also had the most mobile labor force".
  Source: same Epstein PDF (opened)
- Claim: Edward III's 1331 letter protected a Flemish weaver who came to practise and teach.
  Quote (Gentleman's Magazine 1853, translating Rymer's Foedera, vol. 2, p. 823, 5 Edw. III): "John Kempe of Flanders, weaver of woollen cloths, hath come to dwell within our kingdom of England for the purpose of practising his craft therein, and of instructing and informing such as might desire to learn it of him, and hath brought with him certain men and servants, and apprentices to the said trade, we have taken the same John ... under our protection"; and "we promise to cause similar letters of protection to be issued to other men of the same craft, and to dyers and fullers, who wish to come from parts beyond the seas".
  Source: https://lakesguides.co.uk/html/LakesTxt/g853a614.htm and https://lakesguides.co.uk/html/LakesTxt/G853A613.htm (opened; a nineteenth-century antiquarian transcription, not the Foedera itself). The letter text says nothing about a number of masters or a duty to take English apprentices; those first-pass details are struck.
- Claim: Huguenot silk weavers brought skills English workers lacked, and taught locals.
  Quote (Victoria County History, Middlesex vol. 2, 1911, "Silk-weaving"): "The strangers were skilled weavers from Lyons and Tours, who set up their looms in Spitalfields and there manufactured in large quantities lustrings, velvets, brocades, satins ... which previously could only be procured from the famous looms of France. The refugees soon taught the people of Spitalfields to produce these and other goods of the finest quality for themselves, and their pupils soon equalled and even excelled their teachers."
  Source: https://www.british-history.ac.uk/node/15309 (opened)
- Claim: size of the Huguenot influx.
  Quote (London Museum): "It's thought that 40,000-50,000 Huguenots arrived in England between 1660 and 1714 alone. Roughly half are estimated to have settled in what is now Greater London."
  Source: https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/the-huguenots-london/ (opened)

### 1.2 Apprenticeship terms and costs
- Claim: terms and payments in England.
  Quote (Wallis, LSE WP 22/07): "By the seventeenth century in England, apprentices' families often paid a fee or 'premium' to the master at the time of binding and gave bonds for their behaviour and honesty"; "Most apprentices, however, still lived as part of their masters' households ... They were rarely paid wages"; "In England, seven years was set as the legal minimum in 1563; shorter terms of three to five years seem [common elsewhere, text continues]"; "as guilds weakened in England in the later eighteenth century, terms declined from an average of six or seven years to four years across a large range of trades."
  Source: https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/Research/FACTS/WorkingPapers/2007/2207Wallis.pdf (opened)
- Claim: apprentices' labour paid for part of their keep and training.
  Quote (Wallis): "London tilers' apprentices earned 11d a day without food in 1589, well above the 2.56d. necessary to provide a basic" keep; "the costs of supplying this training were low enough that they could be met by the value of less-skilled apprentices' work (at times in combination with a premium) ... Premodern apprenticeship was not a two-stage process of costly training followed by repayment, but a time when work and training were often indistinguishable."
  Source: same Wallis PDF (opened)
- Claim: statutory term length was negotiable and entry rules were relaxed when a shortage hit.
  Quote (Epstein p. 689): "Governments lifted guilds' entry requirements if epidemics or other events reduced the supply of craftsmen." and "the significant differences in the length of apprenticeships between similar crafts suggest moreover that statutory length was an arbitrary and negotiable benchmark".
  Source: Epstein PDF above (opened)
- Claim: apprenticeship was often incomplete.
  Quote (Minns and Wallis abstract): "Many apprentices did not complete their terms of indenture; late arrival and early departure from the master's household were widespread."
  Source: https://ehs.org.uk/article/rules-and-reality-quantifying-the-practice-of-apprenticeship-in-early-modern-england1 (opened, abstract only)
- Claim: London premiums and wages, mid-1700s, from The London Tradesman (1747). The paper is an unpublished-looking working paper titled "Market Forces Shaping Human Capital in Eighteenth-Century London"; the PDF does not name its journal or authors in the text I read, so the first pass's "Campbell, EcHR 2015, using Humphries data" is not confirmed. The data are Robert Campbell's 1747 manual.
  Quote: "Typical earnings for journeymen reported by Campbell range between twelve and fifteen shillings a week while a common laborer might earn five to seven shillings." "The difference between these numbers and the premium levels in Table 1, which range between £10 and £20 for most trades, represent the net pecuniary benefits of apprenticeship". Table 4 (seven-year term from age 14, to age 40): present value of the extra earnings runs from 8 to 53 pounds across annual differences of 12 to 24 pounds and discount rates of 25% to 15%. Table 1 total: mean journeyman wage 15 shillings a week, mean premium 13 pounds, mean set-up cost 351 pounds. Finding: premiums have "a unitary elasticity with respect to wages".
  Source: https://tzin.bgu.ac.il/~kvdbeek/papers/Campbell%20EcHR_2015.pdf (opened)
- Claim: Roman Egypt, weaving contract P. Oxy. 725 (AD 183), five years.
  Quote (Attalus translation): "for the first two years and for seven months of the 3rd year Heraclas shall pay no wages for the boy, but in the remaining five months of the said 3rd year Heraclas shall pay as wages of the said apprentice 12 drachmas a month, and in the 4th year likewise for wages 16 drachmas a month, and in the 5th year likewise 24 drachmas a month"; the boy is "fed by Ischyrion" (his guardian); "twenty holidays a year"; absences are made up; "the one who violates them shall forfeit ... a penalty of 100 drachmas and to the Treasury the like amount." Heraclas agrees "to instruct the apprentice fully in the aforesaid art in the space of five years as far as he knows it himself".
  Source: https://www.attalus.org/docs/select1/p14.html (opened). Note the fifth-year rate is 24, not 20 as one search snippet had it.
- Claim: Roman Egypt, P. Oxy. 275 (AD 66), one year.
  Quote (Wikipedia summary of Grenfell and Hunt): "Ptolemaeus should pay Tryphon an allowance of 5 drachmae a month for food and 12 drachmae at the end of the year for clothing; that Thoonis should serve his full year, and should make up at the end of it any days" missed; "Money penalties are imposed on failure to fulfil these terms." Secondary summary, the translation itself not opened.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Papyrus_Oxyrhynchus_275&action=raw (opened)
- Claim: slaves were apprenticed under the same contract type.
  Snippet only: "Enslaved children could also become apprentices, and their contracts were of the same type as those written for free-born boys." (University of Oslo news page, via search summary, not opened.) P. Oxy. 1647 text not opened; "a woman apprentices her own slave for four years" rests on a search summary of the Oxyrhynchus Papyri vol. 14 index (athenapub.com page, returned 503 when fetched). Treat as snippet only.
  Source: https://hf.uio.no/ifikk/english/research/news-and-events/news/2014/2000-year-old-youth-organization.html and https://athenapub.com/aria1/__southport/__oxypap14c.html (both snippet only)

### 1.3 Buying skilled slaves
- Claim: in Diocletian's Edict (301 AD) a skilled adult slave's price cap was twice a generic adult slave's.
  Quote (Scheidel 2008, Table 3, in units where 1 litre of wheat is 100): "Adult slave (generic) 258,600-387,900"; "Adult slave (skilled) 517,200-775,800". Both are edict price caps, not market prices; Egypt's observed "Adult slave (all)" is 533,980 in the same units.
  Source: https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/AUTHORS/Scheidel2008-Wages.pdf (opened)
- Claim: slave purchase prices varied widely against unskilled wages.
  Quote (Guthmann and Scheidel 2025 abstract): "at different times and places, a typical slave could be purchased for prices equivalent to wages paid from 150 to 1000 days of unskilled labor."
  Source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/exehis/v97y2025ics0014498325000361.html (opened, abstract)
- No source found for owners systematically training house-born slaves (vernae) or for the cost of training slaves.

## 2. Skill premium: levels, series, and speed of closing

### 2.1 England after the Black Death (building craftsman vs labourer, southern England)
Data: Phelps Brown and Hopkins daily money wages as tabulated by Munro (Table 2, quinquennial means). Ratio computed by me from the table (master divided by labourer):
- 1286-90 to 1346-50: master 3.0d (3.3d to 4.0d at the 1301-1335 peak), labourer 1.5d (up to 2.0d); ratio 2.00 throughout, premium 100%.
- 1351-55: 3.6d and 1.8d, ratio 2.00.
- 1356-60: 4.6d and 2.6d, ratio 1.77, premium 77%.
- 1361-65 to 1396-1400: 5.0d and 3.0d, ratio 1.67, premium 67%.
- 1401-05: 5.1d and 3.2d, ratio 1.59. 1406-10: 5.8d and 3.8d, ratio 1.53, premium 53%.
So the premium closed from 100% to 67% over about fifteen years after the plague (labourers' money wage doubled from 1.5d to 3.0d, craftsmen's rose two thirds from 3.0d to 5.0d), then to about 53% over the next forty-five years. It did not rise at first. The craftsman did not gain a bigger premium from scarcity here; the labourer gained relatively.
  Source: https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/15748/1/MPRA_paper_15748.pdf (Munro, "Before and after the Black Death", 2004, Table 2; opened)
- Corroboration (Munro, UT-ECIPA-MUNRO-00-03 and WP 27): "In England, before the Black Death, the journeyman consistently earned only 50 percent of his master's daily money wage; but just after the Black Death, from the mid-1350s, the journeyman's relative daily wage rose to reach 60 percent of his master's rate in the 1360s, maintaining that ratio until just after 1400; and by 1411, it had risen to a new plateau of 66.67 percent".
  Source: https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/public/workingPapers/tecipa-225-1.pdf (opened)
- Claim: the 3:2 craftsman to labourer ratio persisted for centuries from about 1410.
  Quote (Munro quoting Phelps Brown and Hopkins): "the 3:2 ratio between the money wages for masters and labourers, over a five-century period from ca. 1410, underwent 'no sustained change until the First World War'".
  Source: same tecipa-225-1 PDF (opened). Allen's data in 2.2 agree for southern England in 1500-1849 (about 1.5).
- Claim: statutory and city caps.
  Quote (Munro WP 00-03): the Statute of Labourers set "summer wages ... for master masons, carpenters, and tilers, 'without meat or drink', at 3d per day; for their servants and labourers, at 1.5d a day; but it also permitted a rate of 4d per day for master free-masons"; London in 1350 fixed "6d per summer and 5d per winter day ... double that permitted in the 1349 royal Ordinance and the 1351-52 national statute"; "certainly by the 1370s, most employers of building craftsmen in London were ignoring not only the Statute of Labourers ... but also the local civic ordinances on maximum wages".
  Source: https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/public/workingPapers/UT-ECIPA-MUNRO-00-03.pdf (opened)
- Claim: some skilled workers were paid above the statute.
  Quote (Munro 2004): "some craftsmen, evidently senior and/or more skilled, received wages that were 25 percent to 50 percent in excess of permitted rates."
  Source: MPRA PDF above (opened)
- Claim: real wages fell before the plague and did not rise at first.
  Quote (Munro 2004): master craftsmen's "real wages suffered a sharp fall, indeed they fell quite steeply before the Black Death, with the initial recovery of the price level from 1344, and especially with the inflation that soared immediately after the Black Death, one that endured for a full generation." The table shows master real wage index 49.1 in 1346-50 and 46.5 in 1351-55, 62.3 in 1371-75, 77.3 in 1376-80 (base 1451-75 = 100).
  Source: MPRA PDF above (opened)

## 3. What this implies (inference, labelled)
- No series I opened shows a craftsman premium above the pre-plague 100% in England after 1348. The post-plague premium fell. Peak premiums above the long-run level that I did find are one-off import prices (Murano glassmaker, five to ten times home earnings) and the edict's doubling of a skilled slave's price cap.
- The measured closing speed for England: 100% to 67% in about 15 years, to 53% in about 60 years. This reflects labourers' wages catching up as well as craft entry, so it is not a pure entry-driven closing of a craft shortage.
- Long-run premium after 1500 sits near 50% in northwest Europe and near 100% in parts of southern, central and eastern Europe and in Roman Egypt.
- The premium relates to training: Campbell's London data show premium paid by parents is below the capitalised lifetime gain at 15% to 25% discount rates (stated finding: "did not exceed 25% per annum").
